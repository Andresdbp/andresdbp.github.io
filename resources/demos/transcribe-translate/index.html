Honest limits

This gene is a shortened one. Real HBB is about 1600 bp with three exons and two introns of 130 and 850 bases — the introns are far bigger than the exons, which is normal for a human gene. Everything here is scaled down so it fits on a screen, but the first 27 codons and the GU…AG splice signals are real.

What is simplified. The promoter, the TATA box, transcription factors and the termination signal are not modelled — transcription just starts at base 1 of the gene and stops at the end. One polymerase is drawn; a busy gene carries many at once. The 5' cap and the poly-A tail are drawn as symbols, not as sequence, and they are added by the cell rather than copied from the DNA. Splicing is decided here by the GU…AG ends alone; a real spliceosome also needs a branch point and a polypyrimidine tract, and can use different exon combinations (alternative splicing) from the same gene. Exon and intron boundaries are placed on whole codons here; in real genes an intron often interrupts a codon. The standard genetic code is used — mitochondria and a few organisms read a handful of codons differently. And this is a eukaryote: a bacterium has no nucleus, no splicing, and ribosomes that start translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed.

What this is for

The misconception: students collapse transcription and translation into one blurry process called "making a protein". Ask them which strand is read and they say "the DNA". Ask which direction anything is built and they shrug. Ask where it happens and they say "in the cell". Those three questions have exact answers, and the answers are what the whole unit is about.

Which strand. A gene has two strands and only one of them is copied. RNA polymerase reads the template (antisense) strand and builds an RNA that is complementary to it — which means the RNA ends up matching the other strand, the coding (sense) strand, with U wherever that strand has T. Step through transcription one base at a time and watch the readout: template C gives RNA G, template A gives RNA U, template T gives RNA A. Then compare the mRNA with the top row of the DNA. Identical, except T became U. That is why the top strand is called the sense strand — and it is also why students who "read the top strand" get the right letters for the wrong reason and then fall apart the moment a question asks them to write the template.

Which direction. The two strands run antiparallel, so if the coding strand reads 5'→3' left to right, the template reads 3'→5' left to right. The polymerase moves left to right in this picture, which means it is travelling along the template from that strand's 3' end toward its 5' end — and every new nucleotide is added to the 3' end of the growing RNA, so the RNA itself is built 5'→3'. Same rule at the ribosome: the mRNA is read 5'→3', and the polypeptide grows from its N-terminus toward its C-terminus. Nothing in a cell is ever built 3'→5'.

Where. The DNA never leaves the nucleus. Watch it stay there for the whole run while a copy goes out. Transcription, splicing, capping and tailing all happen inside; only the finished mRNA is exported through a nuclear pore; and the ribosome that reads it is out in the cytoplasm. That separation is precisely what an intron is for. The pre-mRNA here is 178 nucleotides, the same length as the gene. Fifty-seven of those nucleotides are intron, and they are cut out before export, so what arrives at the ribosome is 121 nucleotides. Transcription copies everything; the cell edits afterwards. In a bacterium, which has no nucleus, there is no room for that step — and, accordingly, essentially no introns.

Where the frame comes from. The reading frame is not "start at the beginning". The first 18 bases of this mRNA are a 5' UTR and are never translated; the ribosome scans until it finds the first AUG and counts in threes from there. Edit the sequence and the frame is re-derived every time. That is what makes the presets bite: Delete one base removes a single nucleotide from exon 2 and every codon downstream is misread, producing a stop codon early and a 18-amino-acid fragment instead of a 27-amino-acid protein. Break a splice site changes one G to an A at the start of intron 1, so the spliceosome cannot see the intron, the intron stays in the message, and the protein is wrecked from residue 8 onward. That is not a made-up scenario — splice-site mutations in this exact gene cause β-thalassemia.

And the smallest change of all. Press Sickle-cell. One base changes, A to T, in the seventh codon: GAG becomes GTG, and glutamate becomes valine. Length unchanged, every other amino acid unchanged, splicing unchanged. That single substitution is sickle-cell disease. Compare it with Delete one base, where the protein is destroyed, and you have the difference between a substitution and a frameshift in one click.

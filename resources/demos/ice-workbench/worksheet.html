1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. The point is to have something committed to paper that the demonstration will later settle one way or the other.

Prediction

A sealed flask at 425 °C contains 0.100 mol L−1 HI and nothing else — no H2, no I2. For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2 HI(g), K = 54.3 at that temperature, which is a large number.

Does x come out positive or negative, and is [HI] at equilibrium higher or lower than the 0.100 you started with?

4When the “x is small” shortcut fails

Assuming the consumed concentrations barely move turns a quadratic into a square root. It is a legitimate move, and it comes with a condition.

  1. Set up H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2 HI again with [H₂]₀ = 0.1000, [I₂]₀ = 0.1000, [HI]₀ = 0.0000 and Back to the reference K.
  2. Open the Exact vs shortcut tab and record the first row below.
  3. Press Move K until it is safe, record the second row; then press Move K until it breaks and record the third.

a. Press Back to the reference K again and look at the Side by side table. What does the Shortcut column give for [H2], and what does the row Q of that answer report for that column? Explain in one sentence what has gone wrong physically.

b. Still at the reference K, read the Multiply it out panel. What degree is the equation in x, how many roots does it report, and on what grounds is one of them thrown out? Note carefully whether the rejected root is a negative number.

c. With H2 and I2 in the flask, does the shortcut improve as K rises or as K falls? Now press Products only and answer the same question. Why is there no single rule about the direction?

The K slider is a thought experiment, not a laboratory control. Read the note directly underneath it: the only thing that changes K for a given reaction is temperature, and for some settings the note will tell you that no temperature produces that K at all.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away from it. Show your working. Then reopen it, set each situation up, and check yourself — and where you disagree, find the step where it happened.

1. H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2 HI(g), K = 54.3. A flask starts with 0.200 M H2, 0.200 M I2 and no HI. Find x and all three equilibrium concentrations. (Take a square root rather than expanding the quadratic, and say why you are allowed to.)

2. N2O4(g) ⇌ 2 NO2(g), K = 0.0046 at 25 °C. A flask starts with 0.100 M N2O4 and no NO2. (a) Solve for x with the “x is small” shortcut. (b) Apply the 5% check. (c) If it fails, solve the quadratic properly and report the percent error the shortcut would have carried.

3. For H2 + I2 ⇌ 2 HI with K = 54.3, a flask holds [H2] = 0.0500 M, [I2] = 0.0500 M and [HI] = 0.400 M. Without solving for x, compute Q and state the direction of reaction, the sign of x, and whether [H2] rises or falls.

4. Starting from 0.100 M H2, 0.100 M I2 and no HI, someone writes the change row as −x, −x, +x, solves, and obtains concentrations that satisfy K = [HI]2 / ([H2][I2]) exactly. Explain why the answer must still be rejected, and name the quantity that is not conserved by it.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The Le Chatelier tab is untouched by everything above.

  • On the Le Chatelier tab choose Add a catalyst and drag Catalyst speed-up from 1 to 25. Watch the K before and K after readouts and the ends of the solid and dashed curves. Decide exactly what a catalyst does change.
  • Choose Change volume. Predict the shift from the Δn gas readout before you look, first for H2 + I2 ⇌ 2 HI and then for N2 + 3 H2 ⇌ 2 NH3.
  • Choose Change temperature — the one stress that moves K itself. Compare an exothermic reaction with the endothermic N2O4 ⇌ 2 NO2: which way does K go when each is heated, and how does the sign of ΔH° predict it?
  • Choose Add product on the water-gas shift. Does the species you added come back down to the value it had before the stress? Say what that means for the phrase “the system opposes the change”.

Honest limits

What is simplified here. K is written from concentrations in mol L⁻¹; a real thermodynamic K is built from activities and carries no units, which is why K is printed here as a bare number. The Le Chatelier tab drives the approach to equilibrium with rate = kf [reactants]coefficients − kr [products]coefficients, which assumes the reaction happens in one elementary step — most do not, so treat the time axis as qualitative and unitless. What is not simplified: the endpoint. The system stops exactly where Q = K, every time. K against temperature uses the van ’t Hoff equation with ΔH° held constant. All four reactions are all-gas, so a volume change moves every concentration; a pure solid or liquid would not appear in K at all.

What this is for

The misconception: students treat an ICE table as a form to fill in rather than a piece of bookkeeping. Three specific habits follow, and each one has its own tab here.

1. They guess the sign of x. Almost everyone writes −x under the reactants and +x under the products every single time, because that is how the first worked example looked. But the reaction runs whichever way it has to in order to reach K. Compute Q first. If Q < K there is not enough product yet and the reaction runs forward, so x is positive. If Q > K there is too much product and the reaction runs backward, so x is negative and the reactants go up. Press “Products only” and watch every sign in the change row flip. Nothing else on the page decides this — only Q against K.

2. They ignore the coefficients. In H2 + I2 ⇌ 2 HI, two HI molecules appear for every one H2 consumed, so the change row is −x, −x, +2x. Tick the “common wrong answer” box: writing +x there gives [HI] = 0.0880 instead of 0.157 mol L⁻¹. The unnerving part is that the wrong answer still satisfies the K expression — it feels right on the calculator. What it breaks is the atom bookkeeping. Then switch to CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2, where every coefficient is 1 and the mistake changes nothing at all. That is exactly why the habit survives: students practise on reactions that forgive it.

3. They use the “x is small” shortcut without ever checking it. On the default settings the shortcut is off by 368%. Drag K down and watch the error curve fall through the 5% line; drag it up and watch it blow past 100%. Which direction helps is not a rule you can memorise: with H2 and I2 in the flask the shortcut needs a small K, and with HI in the flask instead it needs a large one. The shortcut is safe when x is genuinely small next to the concentration you are subtracting it from — nothing else.

And the three Le Chatelier traps: a catalyst never shifts anything. It speeds up the forward and reverse directions by the same factor, so the system arrives at exactly the same place sooner — the solid and dashed curves end on identical lines. Temperature is the only stress on that panel that changes K itself; adding a substance or squeezing the container moves Q while K sits still. And squeezing only shifts anything when the two sides hold different numbers of moles of gas: H2 + I2 ⇌ 2 HI has 2 mol on each side, so halving the volume doubles every concentration and leaves Q at 54.3 — identical to K, and nothing moves.

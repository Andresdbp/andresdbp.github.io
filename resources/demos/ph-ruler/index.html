Read the fine print

Everything here assumes 25 °C. Kw is only 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at that temperature. Heat the water and Kw gets bigger: at 50 °C it is 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴, so pure water there has pH 6.63 — and it is still perfectly neutral, because [H3O+] still equals [OH−]. Neutral means the two are equal, not “pH 7”.

This ruler covers pH 0 to 14 only. Concentrated strong acids and bases really do go below 0 and above 14; they are just off this scale. And this tool converts between four numbers — it does no equilibrium chemistry, so it will not give you the pH of a weak acid from its concentration.

What this is for

The misconception: pH looks like a rating out of 14, so students read pH 3 and pH 6 the way they would read a 3 and a 6 on a quiz — both lowish, three apart, not a big deal. That reading is wrong by a factor of a thousand.

What to notice: leave this solution at pH 3.00 and the comparison at pH 6.00. On the ruler that is three small steps. Now look at the bars underneath: they are drawn on a straight scale, and the pH 6 bar is one thousandth the length of the pH 3 bar — less than a single pixel, so the demo holds it at one pixel just to keep it visible. Every one pH unit along the ruler multiplies or divides [H3O+] by exactly 10. That is why seawater dropping from pH 8.2 to 8.1 is reported as a 26% jump in acidity, not a 1% one.

Where this model stops: the whole page is fixed at 25 °C. The rule [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ holds no matter how concentrated or dilute the solution is — but it does not hold at other temperatures. Water at 50 °C is still perfectly neutral at pH 6.63; heating it just moved the neutral point. So if you ever see “pH 7 is neutral” with no temperature attached, remember it is only true at 25 °C.

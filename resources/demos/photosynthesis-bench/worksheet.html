1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. Getting it right is not the point; having something on paper to argue with later is.

Prediction

A C3 leaf sits at 25 °C in air at 415 ppm CO2. You raise the light intensity steadily from deep shade (about 150 µmol photons m−2 s−1) all the way to full midday sun (2000). Describe what the rate of carbon fixation does across that range — does it climb in proportion the whole way, level off, or something else?

If you said it levels off, name the thing you think decides how high the level is. If you said it does not, say why not.

a. One of those two rates went to zero the instant the light died and the other did not. Which was which, and what does that difference tell you about where photons are actually consumed?

b. While the light was off, what was paying for the carbon that was still being fixed? Answer in terms of the two pool readouts, not in general terms.

c. Carbon fixed (gross) and Net CO2 exchange differed by a fixed amount in the lit column. What is that amount, and what process does it represent?

The pools on this bench hold 450 µmol ATP and 300 µmol NADPH per m2, roughly a hundred times what a real chloroplast carries. That is deliberate, and the demonstration says so: it slows the drain down to something you can watch. A real leaf stops fixing carbon within a second or two of the light going out. Do not quote the time you measured as a fact about a real plant — quote the shape of what happened.

a. Compare your Carbon fixed (gross) column against the two ceiling columns row by row. State the arithmetic rule that turns the two ceilings into the rate. It is one short sentence, and it is not an average and not a product.

b. Between 150 and 2000 the light ceiling roughly triples but the fixation rate does not. Say in your own words what the light response curve does across this range and what is holding the top of it down.

c. The ATP pool column changes character partway down the table. Describe what a nearly full pool tells you about which ceiling is binding, and what a near-empty pool tells you. Then explain the mechanism: why does a pool that is nearly full force the light reactions to slow down?

d. Go back to your prediction in Part 1. Which parts of it held up, and which specific assumption turned out to be incomplete?

The 30-second wait is not the page being slow. Four Calvin enzymes — GAPDH, FBPase, SBPase and PRK — are switched on by light through the ferredoxin–thioredoxin system, and that switching takes tens of seconds in either direction. It is the reason the CO2 ceiling drifts for a while after you move the light slider even though you never touched the CO2.

4Working backwards, and a shortcut that fails

A limiting-factor question rarely hands you the answer. It hands you a change and asks what it will do.

Working backwards. The bench models rubisco's carboxylation capacity as

ceiling = Vc × Ci / (Ci + Km) × activation,   with Vc = 40 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1 at 25 °C, Km = 300 ppm, and Ci = 0.70 × Ca.

  1. Press Reset. Read Light reactions can supply and Calvin enzymes active, and write both here: __________ and __________.
  2. On paper, work out the Ca (the CO2 around the leaf setting) that would make the CO2 ceiling equal that light ceiling, leaving light and temperature where they are. Show the algebra.
  3. Set the CO2 around the leaf slider to your answer, to the nearest 5 ppm, and read What is limiting right now.

a. What did What is limiting right now say, and how far was your calculated Ca from making the two ceiling numbers match on screen?

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Then reopen it, set the stated conditions, and check yourself — and where you were off, write down which step went wrong.

1. A leaf is at 1000 µmol photons m−2 s−1, 25 °C, and Ca = 500 ppm. Use Vc = 40, Km = 300 ppm, Ci = 0.70 Ca, and an enzyme activation of I/(I + 50). The light reactions can supply 25.9 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1 under these conditions. Find Ci, the CO2 ceiling, the gross fixation rate, and which input is limiting.

2. A leaf is fixing carbon at a steady 22.0 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1. Using 4 e− → 1 O2 + 2 NADPH + 3 ATP, and 3 ATP + 2 NADPH spent per CO2 fixed, calculate (i) the rate of O2 evolution, (ii) the electron flow through the thylakoid, and (iii) the rate at which the Calvin cycle is consuming ATP. Then explain why this stoichiometry forces O2 out to equal CO2 in at steady state, and name one real process the bench leaves out that would break that 1:1 ratio in an actual C3 leaf.

3. The ATP pool on the bench holds 450 µmol m−2 and sits 93.8% full while the leaf fixes carbon at 18.7 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1. The light is switched off, so nothing refills it. (i) Calculate the rate of ATP consumption. (ii) Calculate how long the pool would last if the Calvin cycle kept turning at that same rate until the pool was empty. (iii) The measured time in Part 2 was longer than your answer to (ii). Explain why, referring to what happens to the rate of fixation as the pool falls.

4. Mitochondrial respiration on the bench is 1.2 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1 at 25 °C with a Q10 of 2.0. At 45 °C, 1000 µmol photons and 415 ppm, gross fixation is 4.64 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1. (i) Calculate respiration at 45 °C and the net CO2 exchange, and say what the sign of your answer means the leaf is doing. (ii) At 45 °C the light and CO2 supplies outside the leaf are unchanged. Explain, in terms of the two ceilings, what has actually gone wrong — and which of the two is the heat-sensitive one.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded, and the bench does considerably more than this worksheet used.

  • Take the temperature from 25 to 35 °C at 1000 µmol photons and 415 ppm and watch What is limiting right now. It flips. Work out which ceiling rose and which barely moved, and why warming would do that to one enzyme system and not the other.
  • Press Night, leave it there for two or three minutes, and watch Calvin enzymes active rather than the pools. Then press Lights back on and time how long the rate takes to recover. What does that lag say about a leaf under a cloud that passes overhead every few seconds?
  • On the Live trace plot, the NADPH line is drawn as a wide band with the ATP line on top of it. Set any condition you like and see whether you can ever get the two apart. Then read the stoichiometry on the chloroplast diagram and explain why not.
  • Read the panel headed Read this before you trust the numbers. Pick one thing it says is not modelled and work out what the Rate vs light plot would look like if it were.

Read this before you trust the numbers

“Dark reactions” is a bad name and this bench is built to prove it. The Calvin cycle is light-independent in exactly one narrow sense: a photon is never a substrate of any of its reactions. It is not light-avoiding, it does not prefer night, and it does not wait for the sun to go down. It runs while the light reactions are running, in the same chloroplast, at the same moment — because it is spending the ATP and NADPH they are making right then. On top of that, four Calvin enzymes (rubisco activase, GAPDH, FBPase, SBPase and PRK) are switched on by light through the ferredoxin–thioredoxin system. That is the “Calvin enzymes active” tile. In the dark they switch off over minutes, so a real chloroplast at night is not just out of fuel — the machinery is powered down too.

What is simplified. The two ceilings combine as a minimum, following Blackman: the rate is set by whichever input runs out first, not by multiplying the inputs together. The carrier pools here hold 450 µmol ATP and 300 µmol NADPH per m², roughly a hundred times a real chloroplast’s, purely so the drain is slow enough to watch — a real leaf stops fixing carbon within a second or two of the light going out. Stoichiometry is the AP-level one: 4 electrons → 1 O2 + 2 NADPH + 3 ATP, and 3 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO2 fixed, so supply and demand match exactly and both pools move together; real linear flow makes slightly less ATP than that, and cyclic electron flow around photosystem I makes up the difference. Photorespiration is not modelled, so there is no O2-versus-CO2 competition at rubisco and no CO2 compensation point — that is a separate topic, and leaving it out is what lets the low-light curves lie exactly on top of each other. Stomata are held open at a fixed Ci/Ca = 0.70, so nothing here closes in the heat. C4 and CAM plants are not on this bench at all: CAM separates CO2 uptake (at night) from the Calvin cycle (by day) in time, and C4 separates them in space between two cell types. Neither can be represented by a single steady-state CO2 slider, so pretending otherwise would teach you something false. This is one mesophyll cell of an ordinary C3 leaf.

What this is for

The misconception: somebody once called the Calvin cycle “the dark reactions”, and half of every biology class has concluded that plants do the light reactions in the day and the Calvin cycle at night, like a factory running two shifts. They do not. Both run at once, in the light, in the same chloroplast — and the moment the light goes out the Calvin cycle has only a few seconds of fuel left. The second half of the misconception is quieter and costs more marks: asked which factor is limiting the rate, students reach for whichever input the question mentioned last, or assume that raising any input raises the rate a bit.

What to notice, part one — press Lights out and watch the clock. Oxygen evolution goes to zero instantly: no photons, no water splitting, nothing. But the Calvin cycle does not even flinch. Two seconds into total darkness it is still fixing carbon at 98% of its lit rate, because the ATP and NADPH pools are still nearly full. Four seconds in, still 96%. Then the pools hit bottom and the whole thing falls off a cliff: 64% at six seconds, 32% at eight, 16% at ten, and by 13.7 seconds it is under 5% and done. That is the entire relationship in one experiment. The Calvin cycle does not need light. It needs ATP and NADPH, and the only thing that makes those is the light reactions. Leave it dark and watch the “Calvin enzymes active” tile fall too — the cycle is not merely out of fuel at night, it has been switched off.

What to notice, part two — the pool levels tell you what is limiting. At 1000 µmol photons, 415 ppm, 25 °C, the ATP pool sits at 94% full. Full pools mean the light reactions are making carriers faster than the Calvin cycle can spend them, so the traffic jam is downstream: CO2 is limiting. Now drag light down to 150. The pools crash to 23% and stay there, because now every ATP is spent the instant it appears: light is limiting. You can diagnose the whole system from one bar.

What to notice, part three — the test that separates A from C on the exam. Set light to 150 and read the fixation rate: 9.82 µmol CO2 m⁻² s⁻¹. Now drag CO2 from 415 ppm all the way to 1200. The rate reads 9.82. Not a rounding-error change — no change, exactly zero, because CO2 was never the thing running out. Now put the light at 2000 and repeat: 19.2 → 28.1, a 46% jump, from the identical CO2 move. Same slider, same distance, and it does nothing in one case and a great deal in the other. That is what limiting factor means, and it is why the answer “more CO2 gives more photosynthesis” is marked wrong without the conditions attached. Switch to Rate vs light and you can see it: below about 305 µmol photons the 400 ppm and 1200 ppm curves are the same line. They only separate once the low-CO2 leaf hits its CO2 ceiling and flattens off — and the high-CO2 leaf keeps climbing to a higher plateau. The plateau moved; the initial slope did not.

And temperature is not a third slider like the others. It does not have its own ceiling — it multiplies both of the other two, and it multiplies them differently. Take the temperature to 45 °C: the CO2 ceiling only drops to about 70% of its 25 °C value, but the light-reaction ceiling collapses to 16%, because the thylakoid membranes and photosystem II are the heat-sensitive part. A leaf frying in the sun is not short of light or CO2. It has broken the machine that turns light into ATP.

Honest limits

The picture is nowhere near to scale. If the nucleus drawn here were really this size, the innermost electron shell would be about a kilometre away. An atom is overwhelmingly empty space, and essentially all of its mass is in that tiny dot in the middle.

Shells fill 2, 8, 8 only up to calcium. That is why this page stops at Z = 20. From scandium onward the 3d subshell starts filling before the fourth shell is finished, so the third shell holds up to 18 and the simple 2, 8, 8 rule breaks. The rule is a bookkeeping shortcut for the first twenty elements, not a law.

What else is simplified. Electrons do not orbit in circles — the rings stand in for energy levels, not paths. Isotope masses are real measured values in unified atomic mass units (u), where 1 u is defined as exactly one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, which is why carbon-12 reads 12.000000 exactly. The listed isotopes are the ones that make up natural samples in weighable amounts; you can build nuclei here that do not occur naturally or would fly apart in microseconds, and the page will tell you when you have. Potassium-40 is naturally occurring but radioactive, with a half-life of 1.25 billion years, which is long enough that it is still around and still counted in the average.

What this is for

The misconception: students read 35.45 under chlorine on the periodic table and take it to be a measurement — the mass of a chlorine atom, weighed. It is not. No chlorine atom anywhere has a mass of 35.45 u. Every single one is either about 34.97 u or about 36.97 u. The 35.45 is a bookkeeping average across a population, weighted by how common each kind is, and averages of populations routinely land on values that no individual member has — the same way a class can average 2.3 siblings when nobody has 2.3 siblings.

What to notice. Set the element to chlorine and read the table: 34.969 × 0.7576 = 26.492, and 36.966 × 0.2424 = 8.961. Add them and you get 35.453 u. The answer sits much closer to 35 than to 37, and it sits there for one reason only: three quarters of all chlorine atoms are the light one. Now drag the mixer to 50 / 50 and the average slides to 35.97 — nothing about either nucleus changed, only how many of each you counted. Drag Cl-35 to 100% and the average snaps to 34.969, the mass of one actual atom. That is the test: a number that moves when you change proportions is an average, not a measurement of an object.

Three different jobs, three different particles. Add a neutron to carbon-12 and you get carbon-13. The name stays carbon, the symbol stays C, the square on the periodic table stays put — because the element is defined by the proton count and by nothing else. Take an electron away from sodium and you get Na+: same element, same mass number, different charge. The only way to change the element is to change the number of protons, which chemistry cannot do. That is the difference between a chemical reaction and a nuclear one.

Mass number is not atomic mass. Mass number A is an integer — you are counting particles, and you cannot have 35.45 particles. Atomic mass is a measured quantity in u, and it is never quite the integer either: chlorine-35 is 34.969 u, not 35.000, because binding the nucleus together converts a little of the mass into energy. Two numbers, two units, two meanings. Keep the tiles side by side on this page until they stop looking like the same thing.

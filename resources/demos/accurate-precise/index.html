What this is for

The tempting reading: “precise” sounds like it ought to mean “correct,” and taking more trials and averaging sounds like it ought to walk you toward the true value. Both are reasonable expectations, and both are incomplete — they fail together. A balance that reads 0.30 g high gives 10.30, 10.30, 10.30 g — gorgeously reproducible, and wrong by exactly 0.30 g forever.

What to notice: set the bias to +0.30 g and the scatter to 0.02 g, then run to 500 trials. The green line is the truth. The red line is what this balance actually aims at. The average dives straight onto the red line and stays there. The 95 % band around it collapses as 1/√n — four times as many trials for half the width — but the band is centred on the red line, so the gap to green never closes at all. Averaging is a machine for killing random error, and it is completely blind to systematic error.

Where the model stops short: the demo lets you see the bias because it tells you the true value. In real lab work nobody hands you the truth. That is exactly why you weigh a certified standard, run a blank, or re-titrate a known solution — those are the only ways to catch a bias, and no amount of repeating your unknown will do it. The scatter slider here is also deliberately exaggerated; a working top-loading balance repeats to about ±0.01 g, and the readings are rounded to the 0.01 g the display can actually show.

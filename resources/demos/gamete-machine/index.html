This is not a bookkeeping detail. Human trisomies are traced to MI or MII by genotyping markers near the centromere: three alleles or heterozygosity there means the homologues failed (MI); homozygosity there means sisters failed (MII).

Honest limits

Sister chromatids are only identical proximal to a crossover. Replication makes perfect copies, but a crossover swaps everything distal to the exchange point on one of the two sisters. Set nondisjunction to meiosis II, make it fail on chromosome 1, turn crossing over on and drag the position to 0.50 — the n+1 gamete then holds A and A (proximal, still identical) but B and b (distal, no longer identical). That is exactly why real trisomy studies only trust markers near the centromere.

What is simplified. Two chromosome pairs instead of 23, three tracked genes instead of thousands, and at most one crossover per meiosis — real human bivalents get at least one crossover each and often more. Chromosome 2 carries a single tracked gene here, so a crossover on it would be invisible; only chromosome 1 can show recombinants. Chromosomes are drawn with one long arm carrying every gene. The nondisjunction switch forces the error to happen; in a real cell it is rare, and the spindle assembly checkpoint exists to make it rarer. Which daughter cell fails at meiosis II is fixed here (the one holding the maternal homologue) so that the picture stays comparable when you flip the other controls. Finally, all four products are drawn as gametes: that is true of human sperm, but in human eggs three of the four become polar bodies and meiosis II only finishes if a sperm arrives.

What this is for

The misconception: students learn nondisjunction as one thing — "chromosomes did not separate, so a gamete got an extra one" — and stop there. Asked what is different about a meiosis I error versus a meiosis II error, they say "one is earlier". That is true and useless. The real difference is which two pieces of DNA end up in the same gamete, and it changes both how many gametes are ruined and what alleles the extra chromosome carries.

What to notice. Leave crossing over off for a moment and set nondisjunction to meiosis I on chromosome 2. The two homologues travel to the same pole in anaphase I, so all four gametes come out wrong: two carry three chromosomes and two carry only one. Look at the extra chromosome in an n+1 gamete — it holds D and d, one allele from each parent, because it is holding both homologues. Now switch to meiosis I I. Anaphase I goes perfectly; the error happens one division later, in only one of the two daughter cells. So only two of the four gametes are abnormal — the other two are ordinary, healthy, haploid gametes. And the n+1 gamete now holds D and D: the same allele twice, because those two chromatids are sisters, copies of each other.

Why it works out that way. Meiosis I separates homologues; meiosis II separates sisters. An error in a division can only mis-sort the things that division was supposed to sort. A meiosis I error happens before the cell has divided at all, so every downstream product inherits the mistake — four for four. A meiosis II error happens after the cell has already split in two, so it can only spoil the two products of the one daughter cell that made it — two for four. Same for the alleles: at anaphase I the two things being pulled apart came from different parents, so keeping them together gives you two different alleles. At anaphase II the two things being pulled apart are copies made in S phase, so keeping them together gives you the same allele twice.

The honest caveat, and it matters. "Sisters are identical" is only true for the part of the chromosome between the centromere and the crossover. Make the failure happen at meiosis II on chromosome 1 with a crossover at 0.50 and read the n+1 gamete: A and A at the gene proximal to the exchange, but B and b at the gene distal to it. This is not a quibble invented for a demo — it is why geneticists who want to know whether a real trisomy came from MI or MII only genotype markers close to the centromere, and treat distal markers as scrambled.

And the everyday half of the machine. With no errors at all, this cell still has two independent sources of variation. Flipping the metaphase I arrangement swaps which chromosome 2 rides with which chromosome 1 — two arrangements, 22 = 4 gamete combinations from two pairs, and 223 = 8,388,608 from a human set. Then crossing over goes further: it makes chromatids that neither parent had. Watch the tetrad at prophase I with a crossover between A and B and the four chromatids read A B, A b, a B, a b — all four different, from a cell whose parents supplied only A B and a b. Notice too that a crossover placed at 0.10, proximal to both genes, swaps the colours but produces no new allele combinations at all: recombination is only detectable between markers that the exchange point falls between.

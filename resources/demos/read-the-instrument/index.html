Water wets glass, so its surface is pulled up at the walls and dips in the middle. Read the bottom of the curve, with your eye level with it. Mercury does the opposite — it bulges upward and you read the top.

The rod's lower end is set exactly on the 0.0 mark, so only the upper end has to be estimated. In a careful measurement both ends carry uncertainty and the two combine.

The drawing is schematic: the vertical scale and the depth of the curve are drawn to scale with the marks, the tube width is not. Real glassware also carries a calibration tolerance (a class-A 100 mL cylinder is ±0.5 mL) and a temperature rating; this demonstration ignores both.

What this is for

The misconception: students think a measurement is a number the instrument hands you, and that the last digit is either printed on the glass or a mistake. On a worksheet every reading arrives already written — 24.7 mL — so the only thing left to practise is arithmetic. Then, at the bench, they write 25 mL and throw away a digit they were entitled to, or write 24.73 mL and claim a digit no mark on that tube could support.

What to notice: nothing on this tube says .7. The marks give you the 24; the 7 is you, judging where the bottom of the meniscus sits between two lines — and it is exactly the digit that carries the uncertainty. Now move the graduation slider. The liquid has not moved, but the number of digits you may write changes with the marks: every 1 mL means read to 0.1 mL, every 0.1 mL means read to 0.01 mL. Then move the magnification slider and watch nothing change: a bigger picture of the same marks is still the same marks.

Where this stops being true: the model assumes you read the bottom of the curve with your eye at its level (looking down adds a parallax error the demonstration does not simulate), and it only uses graduations that are powers of ten. Glassware marked every 2 or every 5 units is real, and there the "one estimated digit" rule takes more care.

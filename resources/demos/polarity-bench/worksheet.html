1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. Getting it right is not the point — having it on paper, in ink, so you can compare later, is.

Prediction

CO2 contains two C=O bonds. Oxygen (χ = 3.44) pulls harder on the shared electrons than carbon (χ = 2.55), so each of those bonds is polar. Now suppose you could raise oxygen’s electronegativity to 4.00, the top of the Pauling scale, without changing the molecule’s shape. What happens to the net dipole of the whole molecule: does it grow, shrink, or stay where it is? Give a number if you can.

Write one or two sentences saying what reasoning got you there.

The moment you drag any electronegativity slider, the Measured dipole moment box changes to a dash. That is deliberate: measured values belong to real molecules, and once you have invented an electronegativity there is no experiment to quote. Everything else on the page is still computed honestly from the sliders.

4Working backwards, and a shortcut that breaks

Backwards. A test question rarely hands you the sliders in the order you want them.

  1. Stay on H2O. Using the constant from Part 3, calculate on paper the Δχ that would give a net molecular dipole of exactly 2.00 Δχ units.
  2. From that Δχ, and with χ of O held at 3.44, calculate the χ of H you would need, to two decimal places.
  3. Set the χ of H slider there and record what Net molecular dipole actually reads.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Then reopen it and check yourself; where you differ, find the step that went wrong rather than just correcting the answer. Pauling values you may need: H 2.20, B 2.04, C 2.55, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98, Cl 3.16.

1. A bent AB2 molecule has a bond angle of 104.5° and each A–B bond has Δχ = 1.90. Predict its net molecular dipole in Δχ units. Then state what χ of H you would set on H2O to reproduce this case in the demonstration.

2. The four C–Cl bond dipoles of CCl4 sum to exactly zero. Use only that fact and the χ values above — no trigonometry — to find the net molecular dipole of CHCl3 in Δχ units, and state which end of the molecule the net arrow points towards.

3. Compute Δχ for a B–F bond, then its percent ionic character from Pauling’s relation, % ionic = 100 (1 − e−0.25 Δχ²). BF3 has three of these bonds — the most ionic bonds anywhere in this demonstration — and a net dipole of zero. Explain how both statements can be true at once.

4. The panel What this model leaves out reports that the model comes out 16% below the measured dipole for H2O and 35% below it for NH3, while its predictions of zero for CO2, CCl4 and BF3 are exact and need no calibration. Explain both halves of that: why the model undershoots for those two molecules in particular, and why the zeros are safe from the same error. No calculation required.

6On your own

Nothing here is collected. The demonstration holds more than this worksheet used.

  • Leave Show δ+ / δ− on the atoms switched on and look at CO2. The atoms carry partial charges while the molecule is labelled nonpolar. Settle for yourself what “nonpolar molecule” is and is not claiming.
  • Select HF and find where its marker lands on the Δχ scale relative to the 1.7 line. HF is a covalent gas. Decide what the 1.7 line is actually good for.
  • Compare BF3 and NH3: three bonds each, one flat and one pyramidal. Use Rotate the molecule to convince yourself which is which, then predict the net dipole of each before you read it.
  • In All seven at their real values, read the bond Δχ column against the last column and find every molecule that has polar bonds and is nonpolar anyway.

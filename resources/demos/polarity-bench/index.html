What this model leaves out

These arrows are bond dipoles only. A lone pair on the central atom is a lump of electron density sitting on one side of the atom, and it has a dipole of its own that this model never adds. That is why the sum here comes out too small for exactly the molecules with lone pairs on the central atom:

MoleculeLone pairs on centreThis modelMeasured

The middle column is the vector sum scaled by one single constant, fixed so that HF comes out right (1.028 D per Δχ unit). CHCl3, whose central carbon has no lone pairs, lands within 5%. NH3 is 35% low and it has one lone pair; H2O is 16% low and it has two. The zeros, though, are exact and need no calibration at all: symmetry cancels the lone pairs for the same reason it cancels the bonds.

Other simplifications. Arrow length is set proportional to Δχ, which is a teaching proxy, not a measurement — a real bond dipole is charge times distance, so it also depends on how long the bond is. All bonds are drawn the same length here for clarity; in the real molecules they are not (C=O is 116 pm, O–H is 96 pm). Electronegativities are the standard Pauling values: H 2.20, B 2.04, C 2.55, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98, Cl 3.16. The arrows follow the chemistry convention and point from the δ+ end towards the δ− end; the physics definition of the dipole moment vector points the other way. Molecules are drawn frozen at their equilibrium geometry, and nothing here is time-dependent — every number is computed directly from the sliders.

What this is for

The misconception: students decide whether a molecule is polar by counting. They check the bonds, find that C=O is a polar bond, and conclude that CO2 must be a polar molecule. It is not. CO2 is one of the most stubbornly nonpolar substances in the room, and it contains two genuinely polar bonds — 18% ionic character each. The bonds are not the answer. The molecular dipole is a vector sum, and a sum can be zero even when nothing in it is zero.

What to notice. On CO2, the two bond arrows are 0.89 Δχ units long and point in exactly opposite directions, because the molecule is linear. Lay them head to tail in the second panel and the chain comes back to where it started. Now drag oxygen’s electronegativity all the way to 4.00: both arrows get much longer, each bond becomes far more polar, and the net dipole is still exactly zero. You cannot fix a cancellation by making the things that cancel bigger.

Then press H2O. Same two bonds, both polar, same count. But oxygen has two lone pairs, so the molecule is bent at 104.5° instead of straight, and now the two arrows are not opposite — they are 104.5° apart, and they add to 1.52 units pointing up through the oxygen. The only thing that changed between the two molecules is the angle. Water is a liquid at room temperature, dissolves salt, and sticks to itself because of that angle.

CCl4 against CHCl3 makes the same point without changing the shape at all. Both are tetrahedral, both have bond angles of 109.5°. In CCl4 the four identical arrows point at the four corners of a tetrahedron and sum to zero — spin the molecule and check that it holds from every angle. Swap one Cl for an H and the geometry does not move a degree, but that one arrow is now short and pointing the other way (carbon is more electronegative than hydrogen), so the cancellation fails and you get 0.96 units. Press Make outer atoms identical to turn the H back into a Cl and watch the net dipole collapse to zero in front of you.

Two honest warnings. First: nonpolar does not mean “no charges”. Leave the δ+ / δ− labels switched on while you look at CO2 — the carbon really is δ+ and both oxygens really are δ−. The atoms are charged; the molecule as a whole has no direction. Second: the 0.4 and 1.7 lines on the scale are conventions someone chose, not physics. Select HF and look at where it lands: Δχ = 1.78, past the “ionic” line, and HF is a gas made of discrete covalent molecules, not a salt. The scale underneath is a continuum, which is why it is drawn here as a gradient rather than three boxes.

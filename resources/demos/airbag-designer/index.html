One mole of any ideal gas occupies RT/P litres. The dashed marker is 22.4 L/mol — a single point on this chart, not a property of gases. Move the temperature or pressure slider and watch your bar walk away from it.

Where this model stops being true. The ideal gas law is used throughout; for N2 between 0.8 and 2.5 atm the real molar volume differs from RT/P by well under 1%, so the arithmetic here is honest, but it is still a model. A real inflator is not pure sodium azide: potassium nitrate and silicon dioxide are packed in with it, because the sodium metal in the balanced equation is violently reactive and has to be converted to a harmless silicate glass. That side chemistry makes a little extra gas, so a real cartridge delivers slightly more than the 3:2 ratio alone predicts. The bag also vents through its own fabric and the gas cools within roughly 50 ms, so the hot, high-pressure state you can dial in here lasts only an instant. Sodium azide is acutely toxic; newer inflators use guanidine nitrate instead. Nobody weighs this out by hand.

What this is for

The misconception: students learn 22.4 L/mol early, it works on every homework problem for about a week, and it quietly becomes a universal conversion factor — a fact about gases, like the molar mass is a fact about a compound. It is not. 22.4 L/mol is one measurement of one thing at one set of conditions: 0 °C and 1 atm. The real quantity is RT/P, and it changes every time the temperature or the pressure changes. At 25 °C and 1 atm it is already 24.47 L/mol; at 300 °C and 1 atm, which is the sort of temperature an inflator actually produces, it is 47.0 L/mol — more than double the memorized number.

What to notice: set the mass to 100.0 g and leave it there. At room temperature the bag holds 56.4 L; press Hot deploy and the same 2.307 mol of N2 fills 72.3 L — the identical amount of solid, the identical number of gas molecules, a bag 28% larger. Nothing was created. The molecules just spread further apart. Then watch the dotted outline: that is what 22.4 L/mol would have told you, and it is 8.4% too small at room temperature and 28.6% too small at 300 °C and 1.50 atm. Drag the temperature and pressure sliders until the dotted outline lands back on your bag. You can get there — but only by keeping T/P near 273 K per atm, which is a condition you have to arrange, not a number you get for free.

The other half: the four cards under the bag are the whole calculation, and they run in one direction — grams of solid, moles of solid, moles of gas, litres of gas. The mole ratio 3/2 comes from the coefficients of the balanced equation, not from anywhere else. Press Weigh out exactly enough at room temperature and then at 300 °C: filling the same 60 L bag takes 106.3 g of azide in one case and 82.9 g in the other. How much you weigh out depends on the conditions the gas will be under. That is the connection the calculation exists to make.

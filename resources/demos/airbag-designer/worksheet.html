1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know, on paper, before the screen tells you anything. The point is to have a number to compare against later.

Prediction

An inflator holds 100.0 g of sodium azide, which decomposes completely: 2 NaN3 → 2 Na + 3 N2. The gas in a deployed bag reaches roughly 300 °C at 1.50 atm. How many litres of N2 does the bag hold under those conditions? Commit to a number. (M(NaN3) = 65.01 g/mol.)

In one sentence, say what conversion you used for the last step — moles of N2 to litres — and where that number came from.

2Reading the chain

Open the demonstration and press Reset. The four cards under the bag are one dimensional-analysis chain, left to right. The panels on the right report the same result two ways.

  1. Check that Sodium azide weighed out reads 100.0 g, Gas temperature reads 25 °C, Pressure in the bag reads 1.00 atm, and Driver bag — 60 L is the selected target.
  2. Copy the four card values, in order, into the table.
  3. Then copy the four numbers from the two panels on the right.

3What the last step actually depends on

Keep the mass and the pressure fixed. Only the temperature moves, so the number of moles of N2 is identical in every row.

  1. Press Reset. Confirm the mass is 100.0 g and the pressure is 1.00 atm; do not touch either again during this part.
  2. Drag Gas temperature to each value in the first column.

Every molar volume on this page is RT/P for an ideal gas. The demonstration says where that stops being safe, in the panel below the chart — read it, including the part about what else is packed into a real inflator alongside the azide.

4Running the chain backwards

A design question rarely gives you the mass. It gives you the bag and asks what to weigh out. Weigh out exactly enough solves the chain in reverse, for the conditions currently set.

  1. Press Reset, then press Weigh out exactly enough. Record the mass and the fill percentage (row 1).
  2. On paper first: work out how many grams the same 60 L bag needs at 300 °C and 1.50 atm. Show it in the space below. Then press Hot deploy, press Weigh out exactly enough again, and fill row 2.
  3. Select Passenger — 150 L, press Room temperature, press the button once more, and fill row 3.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Use R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K), M(NaN3) = 65.01 g/mol, and the balanced equation 2 NaN3 → 2 Na + 3 N2. Then reopen the demonstration, dial in each problem, and check yourself.

1. 45.0 g of NaN3 decomposes completely. Find the moles of N2 produced and the volume that gas occupies at 27 °C and 1.00 atm.

2. A 150 L passenger bag has to be filled at 150 °C and 1.20 atm. What mass of NaN3 does the inflator need?

3. Two inflators hold identical masses of NaN3 and both deploy at 1.00 atm — one on a −20 °C morning, one at 40 °C. Without calculating either volume, find the factor by which the second bag is larger, and explain why that factor is not 40 ÷ (−20).

4. 100.0 g of NaN3 is to exactly fill the 60 L driver bag at 1.00 atm. At what temperature, in °C to the nearest degree, does that happen?

6On your own

Nothing here is graded, and the demonstration does more than this worksheet used.

  • 0 °C and 1.00 atm is not the only setting where the shortcut is right. Leave the pressure at 1.50 atm and hunt for a temperature that drives Off by under 0.5%. Then find another pair. What do all of them have in common?
  • The lower chart plots litres per mole across five fixed conditions plus your current setting. Before you move anything, predict where your bar will land relative to the 300 °C, 2.00 atm row, then set the sliders and see.
  • The bag outline is drawn to scale, and width grows as the cube root of volume. Find two settings whose gas volumes differ by a factor of 2 and judge by eye how much wider the second bag is. Check your eye against 21/3.
  • Select the passenger bag, press Cold morning, and press Weigh out exactly enough. Watch what the mass slider does at its upper end, and work out what the design would need instead.

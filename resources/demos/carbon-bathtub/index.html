Stopping the tap empties the nutrient tub in about a week. But the algae that already grew still have to die, and the dead matter already on the bottom still has to be decomposed — and decomposers keep breathing the whole time. Oxygen cannot recover until that third tub has drained.

Honest limits

These are illustrative models, not predictions. The carbon side is a single well-mixed reservoir losing CO2 to one combined sink at a rate proportional to the excess over 280 ppm. The real carbon cycle has several sinks on very different clocks, and a stubborn tail: even at true net zero a large fraction of the added CO2 stays in the air for centuries to millennia, so the drain here is far more forgiving than the real one. This model also lets the sink keep growing without limit as concentration rises, whereas real ocean and land sinks saturate. Both simplifications make stabilisation look easier here than it is.

The lake is not a calibrated limnological model either. It is a stratified summer water column with poor gas exchange, four first-order tubs, and Redfield stoichiometry (41 µg of algal carbon per µg of phosphorus; 2.67 mg of O2 per mg of carbon respired). Real lakes add light limitation, grazers, sediment phosphorus release, mixing events and species change. Oxygen is floored at 0 mg L⁻¹, because below that there is nothing left to consume.

What is not simplified is the arithmetic that matters here: in both modes the stock changes by exactly (inflow − outflow) every step, and nothing else. That is the part worth trusting.

What this is for

The misconception: students hear “emissions fell last year” and conclude that the amount of CO2 in the air fell too. It did not. Emissions are a flow — how fast carbon is being added, in gigatonnes per year. Concentration is a stock — how much is in there right now, in ppm. They are different quantities with different units, and the only thing that connects them is this: the stock rises whenever inflow is bigger than outflow. Not when inflow is rising. When it is bigger. A falling emissions rate that is still above the uptake rate is a bathtub filling more slowly — and a bathtub filling more slowly is still filling.

What to notice, in three moves. First press Run and change nothing. The tap holds perfectly steady at 41 Gt CO2 per year, and the level climbs from 424 ppm anyway, because the drain is only carrying 22.3. Second, press Cut 30%. Emissions fall to 28.7 — a genuinely enormous cut, far bigger than anything achieved so far — and the level still rises, at +0.82 ppm per year, because 28.7 is still bigger than 22.3. Skip fifty years ahead and the concentration has gone up another 26 ppm during the decades when emissions were falling. Third, press Set emissions to today’s uptake. That sets the tap to 22.3, a cut of 45.6%, and only now does the level go flat. That is the number that stops it: not zero, not “less than last year”, but equal to the outflow.

And then the sting. Set emissions back to 41 and read the second target tile. Constant emissions do not give a constant concentration; they give a rising one that eventually settles near 545 ppm, once the drain has grown enough to keep up. Every emissions rate has its own eventual level: 28.7 buys you 465 ppm, 22.3 buys you today’s 424, and 20.5 — the Cut 50% button — is finally below the drain, so the level slowly falls to 412 and stops there. Notice how narrow that window is. Between a 30% cut that still raises the level by 41 ppm and a 50% cut that lowers it by 12 lies the whole difference, and neither of them is “emissions went down”. Choosing an emissions rate is choosing a concentration, decades later.

Why the runoff mode is the same demo. Switch modes and every label changes but no equation does. Fertiliser fills a nutrient tub; the nutrient tub drains into algae; algae drain into dead organic matter; and decomposers burning that dead matter drain the oxygen tub faster than the air can refill it. Set loading to Heavy, 6.0, run until oxygen collapses to 0.8 mg L−1, then press Stop the fertiliser and keep running. The nutrient tub is essentially empty inside a fortnight — and oxygen does not climb back over 5.0 mg L−1 for about 53 days, because 8.2 mg C L−1 of dead algae is still sitting there being decomposed at 3% a day and the decomposers are still breathing. Nothing is being added any more and the lake is still dying. That is stock-and-flow reasoning, and it is why “we stopped doing the bad thing” and “the problem is over” are two entirely different claims.

The honest caveat. Neither model is a prediction. Both use one well-mixed reservoir per tub and a single first-order drain, which is the simplest thing that behaves like a bathtub at all. Real carbon sinks saturate and real lakes have sediment, grazers and seasons. The numbers on screen are there to make the arithmetic of accumulation visible, not to forecast anything.

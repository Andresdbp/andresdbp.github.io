Why the subscripts are locked

A subscript is part of the substance’s name. The 2 in H2O says a water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen. That number came from the chemistry, from what actually bonds to what — it is not yours to adjust. If you “balanced” H2 + O2 → H2O by writing H2O2, you did not balance anything: you replaced water with hydrogen peroxide, which is a different chemical that will bleach your hair and disinfect a cut.

A coefficient is a count. The 2 in 2 H2O means two whole water molecules. It multiplies every atom in the formula unit: 2 H2O is 4 H and 2 O. Brackets are where the count slips — 2 Ca(NO3)2 is 2 Ca, 4 N and 12 O, because the 3 and the 2 multiply before the coefficient does. Set that reaction up and count the circles in the particle view.

Honest limits

This is atom bookkeeping and nothing else. It shows that mass is conserved — the same atoms come out that went in, rearranged — but it says nothing about whether a reaction happens, how fast, how far, or how much energy it takes or gives off. States of matter (s, l, g, aq) and charges are left off so the counting stays visible. The particle boxes are drawn as tidy grids; real molecules are in constant disordered motion and, for the metals here, zinc and copper are not loose single atoms but atoms packed in a lattice. “Formula unit” is used instead of “molecule” for the ionic compounds, because NaNO3 in a solid is a repeating array, not a discrete particle. Every reaction shipped here has exactly one lowest-terms answer.

What this is for

The misconception. When an equation refuses to balance, the tempting repair is the little numbers. H2 + O2 → H2O will not balance, so H2 + O2 → H2O2 gets written instead — and it does balance: two hydrogens, two oxygens, done. The instinct is a reasonable one, because the subscripts are the only numbers in sight that make the count come out. It is also the most common error in this unit, and it is not a small slip. Balancing is supposed to be a statement that atoms are conserved. Editing a subscript quietly swaps in a different substance and then claims the books balance. That is not conservation of mass; it is cooking the books.

What to notice. The subscripts on this page cannot be edited at all. There is no control for them, because in real chemistry there is no such control either — the formula of a compound is determined before you ever write the equation. The only thing you may change is the big number in front, which is a count of whole units. Watch the particle view as you press the plus button: nothing about any molecule changes shape, another identical copy simply appears. That is what a coefficient means, and it is why a coefficient can never break a substance.

Two conditions, not one. Press Double every coefficient on an equation you have already balanced. Every row of the table stays green — the atoms really do still match — and the Check button still rejects it. 4 H2 + 2 O2 → 4 H2O is a true statement about atoms, but 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O says the same thing with nothing wasted, and it is the answer chemists agree to write. The test is a greatest common divisor: if every coefficient divides by the same whole number, divide through.

Where atoms go missing. Brackets. In 3 Ca(NO3)2 the oxygen count is 3 × 2 × 3 = 18, not 3 × 3 = 9 and not 6. The subscript inside the bracket, the subscript outside it, and the coefficient all multiply together. That is why the double-replacement reaction with calcium nitrate and sodium phosphate is on this page: build it, then count the oxygen circles on the left of the particle view and check them against the table. Twenty-six oxygen atoms on each side, in two chemically different homes — nitrate and phosphate — and the equation does not care which home, only how many.

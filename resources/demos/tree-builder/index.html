5 · What the outgroup is doing

The lancelet is not just an extra animal. It is known from other evidence to have branched off before any of the other five, so it does two jobs at once. It roots the tree — it tells you which end is the past, so “shares an ancestor with” has a direction. And it polarises every character — whatever state the lancelet has is taken as the ancestral state, so any other state is derived. Without an outgroup you can still see which animals resemble each other, but you cannot tell which resemblances are new and which are leftovers, and only new ones are evidence of a group.

Shared ancestral does not group. Shark, salamander and lizard all fail to hold a constant body temperature. That is not something they invented together — it is what the common ancestor of all six already did, so it says nothing about which of them are closest relatives. Load Cold-blooded together and count the cost: the tree jumps from 15 steps to 21.

Honest limits

Parsimony is a criterion, not a truth. The fewest-changes tree is the hypothesis that assumes the least, which is a good reason to prefer it — not a guarantee that evolution actually took the short route. Evolution has no obligation to be economical, and when change is fast or branches are very unequal, parsimony can confidently pick the wrong tree. Real phylogenetics uses far more data and probabilistic models, and every published tree is provisional.

What is simplified. Fourteen characters and six taxa; a real analysis uses thousands of aligned DNA sites across hundreds of taxa. All characters are two-state and every change costs the same step, in either direction — real characters have more states and gains and losses are rarely equally likely. Several characters here give exactly the same grouping (bony skeleton, lungs, and limbs with digits all separate the same four animals) only because no ray-finned fish is in the sample; adding a tuna would split them apart. Branch lengths mean nothing here — this is a cladogram, so the horizontal spacing shows the order of branching, not time or amount of change.

What this is for

The misconception: students read a cladogram as a ladder of progress. The animals near the left are “primitive”, the animals near the right are “advanced”, humans belong at the top, and two names printed next to each other must be close relatives. Under that reading a tree is a ranking — a scoreboard of how far each species has climbed.

What to notice, first. Build the best tree you can, then click the node joining lizard and pigeon. The picture flips; the step count does not move, not by one. Click the node above it and human swaps to the other side. Rotate every node on the page and the score is still the same number, because a node is a free hinge: the only information in a tree is which taxa share which ancestors, and rotating changes nothing about that. So “printed next to each other” is not a relationship, and there is no top of the tree — every one of the six tips is a living species with exactly the same amount of evolutionary time behind it.

What to notice, second. The most parsimonious tree of all 105 possibilities needs 15 steps, and in it the human is not the last tip. Human sits on a side branch and lizard and pigeon are more deeply nested. Anyone reading branch position as advancement has just been told that a pigeon out-ranks them. The tree is not saying that, and it never was.

Why the score works. Only characters that are shared and derived carry grouping information. The amniotic egg is derived (the lancelet and shark do not have it) and shared by human, lizard and pigeon, so it is evidence those three had a common ancestor the others did not. Compare the useless ones. Every animal here has a notochord at some stage, so that character costs 0 steps on every tree and separates nobody — it is shared ancestral. Feathers are derived but only the pigeon has them, so they cost exactly 1 step on every tree and cannot prefer one tree over another. Ten of the fourteen characters here do real work; four cannot, and knowing which is which is most of the skill.

The interesting conflict. A constant warm body temperature and a four-chambered heart are both shared by human and pigeon, and both are derived. Group those two and you save 2 steps on those characters — but you then have to break the skull opening, the uric acid waste and the foot scales that lizard and pigeon share, costing 3. The Ladder tree is exactly that trade, and it lands on 16. The data do not agree with themselves, and parsimony is how you decide which conflict to swallow: the tree keeps the evidence supported by three characters and pays for two independent origins of warm-bloodedness. That is a real result — mammals and birds did evolve endothermy separately — and the demonstration got there by counting, not by ranking anybody.

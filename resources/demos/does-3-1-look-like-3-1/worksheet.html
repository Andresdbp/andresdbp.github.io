Does 3:1 Ever Look Like 3:1?

What a Mendelian ratio actually predicts about one batch of offspring, and what chi-square is really asking when you test it.

1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know about monohybrid crosses. Nothing here is graded on being right — the point is to have a number on paper that the demonstration can later confirm or contradict.

Prediction

A cross is exactly 3:1: every offspring has probability 0.75 of the dominant phenotype, with no linkage, no lethal alleles and no scoring error. You count 20 offspring, then repeat that whole count many times over.

Write the lowest and the highest dominant : recessive ratio you expect to see across those repeats.

Now suppose you counted 1000 offspring in each repeat instead. Would that range of ratios be wider, narrower, or the same? Circle one, then say in one sentence how you decided.

4Where the eyeball test fails

A common shortcut is to judge a cross by how far its ratio looks from 3.00:1. Below, that shortcut is put up against the test itself.

  1. Click 20, then 100, then 1000 in the Offspring counted, n row. At each one, read the note at the bottom of the Run the whole experiment again and again panel: it states the range of dominant counts a chi-square test would accept at that n.

a. A cross gives 72% dominant offspring. Write that as counts at n = 100 and again at n = 1000, then compute χ² by hand for each. Use E = 0.75n and E = 0.25n.

b. State the verdict at each sample size against the critical value 3.841, and check both against the accepted-count ranges in your table.

c. The ratio was identical in both cases. Explain why the two verdicts differ, and why that means “this ratio looks close enough to 3:1” is not a test.

Look at the two rejection tiles while you work. About 5% of the replicates get rejected at α = 0.05 even though every one of them came from a cross that is exactly 3:1. The tile marked Exact long-run rate at this n gives the true figure for the n you have selected; it is never a clean 5.00%, because offspring come in whole numbers and the cut can only fall between two adjacent counts.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show every step: expected counts, each (O − E)²/E term, the total, the degrees of freedom, and the comparison with 3.841. Then reopen the demonstration, set n to match, and check your verdict against the accepted-count range in the third panel.

1. A monohybrid cross yields 40 offspring: 28 with the dominant phenotype and 12 with the recessive. Test the 3:1 hypothesis at α = 0.05 and state your conclusion in a full sentence.

2. The same cross is repeated on a larger scale: 400 offspring, 285 dominant and 115 recessive. Test the 3:1 hypothesis at α = 0.05.

3. A third count gives 1000 offspring: 712 dominant and 288 recessive. Test the 3:1 hypothesis at α = 0.05.

4. No calculation for this one. Problems 2 and 3 both gave about 71% dominant, but only one of them was rejected. A lab partner concludes that the 1000-offspring count must have been done badly. Explain what is wrong with that conclusion, and give two things a significant χ² does not tell you.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded, and the demonstration does more than this worksheet used.

  • Drag the Replicate experiments slider from 20 up to 500 and watch the two rejection tiles. One of them jumps around and one of them does not. Work out which is the estimate and which is the target, and how many replicates you need before they agree to within a percentage point.
  • Step through all five values of n reading only the Exact long-run rate at this n tile. It is never exactly 5.00%. Find which n comes closest, and explain the pattern using the fact that offspring counts are whole numbers.
  • At n = 20 the histogram is a comb of evenly spaced bars with gaps between them. Measure the spacing, account for it, and then find where the replicates that fall below 40% dominant end up on that axis.

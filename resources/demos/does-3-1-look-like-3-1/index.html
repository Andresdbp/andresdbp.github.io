Honest limits

Chi-square needs reasonably big expected counts. The usual rule of thumb is at least about 5 expected in every class. At n = 20 the expected recessive count is exactly 5.0 — sitting right on the floor — and the test starts to misbehave there, because the possible outcomes are too chunky. With 20 offspring the recessive count can only be a whole number, so the observed percentage can only land on multiples of 5. A smooth continuous curve is being used to judge a staircase. The p-values at n = 20 on this page are indicative, not exact.

What is simplified. Every offspring is an independent draw at p = 0.75, which assumes complete dominance, no lethal alleles, no linkage to anything, equal survival of all genotypes, and no miscounting. In a real corn ear the kernels are not quite independent and a few are genuinely hard to score. Real deviations from 3:1 do exist and matter — a homozygous-lethal allele gives 2:1, an epistatic interaction can give 9:7, and a scoring bias gives whatever your eyes gave it. This page shows only what pure sampling does, so that when you meet a real 2:1 you can tell it apart from luck. The p-value uses the standard continuous chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom, with no continuity correction applied.

What this is for

The misconception: students believe a monohybrid cross produces a 3:1 ratio, the way a machine produces parts. So when the corn ear comes back 28 purple : 12 yellow, they write “our experiment failed” or “we must have miscounted.” Then, to fix it, they plug the numbers into a chi-square formula they were handed, get a number, compare it to 3.84, and write down a verdict they could not explain to anyone. Both halves of that are the same error: treating a probability as a promise.

What 3:1 actually claims. Each offspring, on its own, has a 3-in-4 chance of the dominant phenotype. That is the whole claim. It says nothing about what any particular batch of 40 will look like, for the same reason that a fair coin makes no promise about the next 40 flips. 28:12 is not a failed 3:1. It is one of the completely ordinary things a real 3:1 cross does. Press Re-run the cross at n = 20 a dozen times and you will personally watch a perfect 3:1 mechanism hand you 12:8, 17:3, 14:6 and 18:2. Nothing changed in the machine between those runs.

What to notice. Set n = 20 and look at the histogram: the results are smeared from about 50% to 100% dominant. Now set n = 1000 — same cross, same axis — and the whole cloud collapses into a narrow spike straddling 75%. The individual offspring did not become more obedient. There are simply more of them, and the spread of a percentage shrinks like 1/√n: the standard deviation falls from about 9.7 percentage points at n = 20 to about 1.4 at n = 1000, a factor of √50 ≈ 7. This is the entire reason your teacher wants you to count the whole ear. A ratio is not a measurement you take once; it is an estimate whose precision you buy with sample size.

What chi-square is actually asking. Not “is my ratio 3:1?” It asks: if the true ratio were 3:1, how weird would data this far off be? The (O−E)²/E terms measure the gap in units of how big a gap you should expect — that is why you divide by E, and why being 2 off is a scandal when you expected 10 and a rounding error when you expected 750. The p-value is the answer: the fraction of honest 3:1 experiments that would look at least this strange.

The two sentences to get right, and both are on the readouts above. First: failing to reject does not prove the ratio is 3:1. Your data were merely unsurprising, and unsurprising data are compatible with lots of hypotheses — a true 0.73 would also have passed. You never prove a null hypothesis; you only fail to knock it over. Second: rejecting does not mean you did the experiment badly. Read the rejection tiles: about 5% of these replicate experiments get rejected at α = 0.05 — roughly 1 in 20 — even though every single one of them came from a cross that is exactly 3:1. That 5% is not a malfunction. It is the number you chose when you chose α = 0.05. Setting the bar at “the strangest 5% of honest experiments” guarantees that 5% of honest experiments get convicted. (The exact rate is not a clean 5.00% — it is 6.52% at n = 20 and 4.23% at n = 40 — because offspring come in whole numbers and the cut can only fall between two adjacent counts. The tile computes the true value for whichever n you pick.)

And the trap on the other side. Because the critical value stays at 3.841 while the expected counts grow, a big n can reject a ratio that is off by almost nothing. A real deviation of one percentage point is invisible at n = 40 and damning at n = 100 000. So a significant χ² means “this gap is too big to blame on luck” — it does not tell you the gap is large, and it does not tell you what caused it. That part is still your job as a biologist.

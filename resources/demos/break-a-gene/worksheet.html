1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. The number does not have to be right — it has to be on paper, so that later you can see exactly which assumption moved.

Prediction

One base is changed at random somewhere in a protein-coding sequence. Out of 100 such changes, how many do you expect to leave the amino acid sequence of the protein exactly as it was?

Write one or two sentences saying how you arrived at that number.

Clicking the same base again cycles to the next possible base, and once more returns it to wild type.

4Where the shortcut breaks, and how to work backwards

“Third-base changes are silent” is a good generalisation and a bad law. An exam question will hand you the exception, or hand you the consequence and expect you to find the change that caused it.

  1. Click any base inside codon 16. Its DNA reads T G G.
  2. Read the 3rd base row of the Codon 16 panel. Write down all three chips exactly as they appear (codon and amino acid), and the row label.

a. One of those three is red. What consequence class is that, and what happens to translation? Give the length of the peptide it produces.

b. Using the codon table, explain why tryptophan in particular has no silent option at any of its three positions, while alanine has three at the third.

Now work backwards from a consequence.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page, or look away from it. Show your working. Then reopen it and check yourself — and where you were wrong, write down which step went off.

1. A coding sequence is 90 bases long.

(a) How many different single-base substitutions are possible in it? Show the arithmetic.

(b) Enumerating all of them gives 69 silent changes, of which 66 come from third-base substitutions. Given that a third of the 90 bases sit in each codon position, what percentage of third-base substitutions are silent, and what percentage of all substitutions are silent?

2. Not one of the 90 possible second-base substitutions is silent. State what would have to be true of the standard genetic code for a second-base substitution to be silent, and say whether any such case exists. This is an explanation, not a calculation.

3. A single-base substitution changes an mRNA codon from UGG to UGA. This is codon 16 of a 30-codon reading frame.

(a) Which position within the codon was hit, and which base replaced which?

(b) Name the consequence class.

(c) How many amino acids long is the mutant peptide, and how many residues are missing relative to wild type?

4. A classmate argues that deleting three bases from a gene must be about three times as damaging as deleting one. Explain why this is backwards. Your answer should say what a one-base deletion does to the codons downstream of it, what a three-base deletion does to those same codons, and which of the two leaves more of the original protein intact.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The demonstration does considerably more than this worksheet has used.

  • Switch the mutation type to Deletion, set How many bases to 1, and click base 40 (the first base of codon 14). Note Peptide length and Residues altered. Now set the size to 3 and click the same base. Try sizes 1, 2 and 3 in Insertion mode as well. Which number of bases behaves unlike the other two, and why is it the frame rather than the count that matters?
  • Press Clear tally, then Run 500 more one press at a time. How many draws does it take before every value in the Share column sits within one percentage point of Exact? The triangles under the bars mark the exact boundaries — watch the bars close on them.
  • Mutate a base in codon 1 and read what the page says about the start codon. Then read the Honest limits panel to the end and find the one claim it makes about silent mutations that this worksheet has quietly ignored throughout.

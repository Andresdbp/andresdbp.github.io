Honest limits

Most missense mutations are tolerated. This page labels a change “missense” when the amino acid is different. That is a statement about the code, not a prediction of disease. Swap one hydrophobic residue for another on the outside of a protein and usually nothing measurable happens; every human carries roughly 10,000 missense variants and is fine. What matters is which residue, where it sits, and what it is replaced by. The sickle-cell change is famous precisely because it is unusual: a charged, water-loving glutamate becomes a greasy valine on the surface of a protein packed at 300 g/L.

Silent does not always mean harmless either. A synonymous change makes the same protein, so it is silent in the sense this page means. In real cells a few synonymous changes still matter — they can alter splicing signals, mRNA folding or how fast the ribosome moves. Rare, but real. Nothing on this page models that.

What is simplified. Real HBB is 147 codons long and split by two introns; this is the first 30 codons of the coding sequence, spliced, with no promoter, no UTRs and no regulatory sequence. Only one mutation is applied at a time. The DNA row is the coding strand — the template strand is its complement, and transcription of the template gives an mRNA identical to the coding strand except that T is replaced by U, which is why those two rows always match. Frameshifted peptides are read to the end of this 90-base window; in the real gene translation would keep going until it hit a stop codon in the new frame. Translation here always begins at base 1 of the window, so if you destroy or displace the AUG the peptide drawn is what would be made if the ribosome started there anyway — a real ribosome scans for the first AUG instead. Amino acid groupings (nonpolar / polar / acidic / basic) are the standard AP simplification of a continuous property.

What this is for

The misconception: students treat every mutation as a coin toss between “broken” and “really broken.” A base changes, so the protein changes, so something goes wrong. They have been told the code is “redundant,” and they can recite that six codons spell leucine, but they file it away as trivia — a quirk of a table they have to memorise. What they never see is that the redundancy is organised, and that the organisation is what decides whether a mutation matters at all.

What to notice. Take codon 4, CTG, leucine. Click its third base: you get CTA, CTC or CTT — still leucine, all three times. The protein is identical. Now click its second base: CAG is glutamine, CCG is proline, CGG is arginine. Three changes, three different proteins, no exceptions. Same codon. Same one-base change. Completely different odds, depending only on which of the three positions you hit.

That is not a fluke of this codon. Press Run 500 more and read the stacked bars. Across this sequence there are exactly 270 possible single-base substitutions — 90 bases × 3 alternatives — and enumerating every one of them gives 69 silent, 193 missense and 8 nonsense: 25.6%, 71.5% and 3.0%. Split that by position and the structure jumps out: 73.3% of third-base changes are silent, and 0.0% of second-base changes are. Not “hardly any.” Zero. There is no pair of codons anywhere in the standard genetic code that differ only at the middle base and still spell the same amino acid. First-base changes sit in between at 3.3%, and every one of those exceptions is either leucine or arginine.

Why the code is built that way. There are exactly eight fourfold boxes — every codon beginning UC, CU, CC, CG, AC, GU, GC or GG — and they ignore the third base entirely. That is 32 of the 64 codons, half the code, where a mistake at the third position costs literally nothing. Switch to the codon table and look for the green row labels: those eight rows have the same amino acid four times across. And chemically similar amino acids are parked in neighbouring rows, so even when a change is missense it is often a conservative one — every single codon with U in the middle position (UUx, CUx, AUx, GUx) spells a hydrophobic amino acid, so hitting the first base of one of those usually swaps one greasy residue for another. The result is a code that absorbs the most error-prone kind of change and quarantines the most dangerous one. Only 3.0% of substitutions here produce a premature stop, and that is the outcome that reliably destroys the protein.

Then there is the indel. Switch to Deletion, size 1, and click almost anywhere: the peptide downstream turns into gibberish and usually crashes into a stop codon within a few residues. Now set size to 3 and click the same base. One amino acid disappears and everything after it is exactly as before. Three bases in or out is not three times worse than one — it is enormously less bad, because the reading frame survives. The frame, not the number of bases, is what matters.

The honest caveat. “Missense” on this page means the amino acid is different, not that the organism is sick. Most missense changes do nothing you could measure. Click the sickle-cell button: codon 7, GAG → GTG, glutamate to valine. It is a single missense change out of the 193 possible ones in this window, and it is the one that causes sickle cell disease — not because missense is destructive in general, but because that particular acidic residue, on that particular surface, was the thing holding haemoglobin apart.

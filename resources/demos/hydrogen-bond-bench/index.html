The equations, in full

Young–Dupré: cos θ = Wadhesion/γ − 1, and since the work of cohesion is Wcoh = 2γ, that is cos θ = 2Wadh/Wcoh − 1.
Jurin’s law: h = 2γ cos θ / (ρ g r), with ρ = 997 kg m⁻³ and g = 9.81 m s⁻². Put those together and the whole thing collapses to h = (2Wadh − Wcoh) / (ρ g r): a straight tug-of-war between the two attractions, divided by the radius.

Honest limits

The density stays at water’s the whole time. This bench changes only the two bond strengths, so the “mercury-like” preset gives you mercury’s contact angle but not mercury’s liquid. Real mercury has a surface tension of 485 mN m⁻¹ (nearly seven times water’s) and a density of 13 534 kg m⁻³ (13.5 times water’s), and those two effects largely cancel: in a 0.80 mm tube real mercury sinks about 14 mm, not the 31 mm drawn here. The direction is right and the contact angle is right; the size of the drop is not.

Also simplified: the tube is perfectly clean and perfectly cylindrical, the liquid is at rest and at equilibrium (Jurin’s law says nothing about how fast it gets there), gravity is the only thing opposing the climb, and the meniscus is treated as part of a sphere — true only while the tube is narrow enough that gravity does not flatten it, which holds across this slider. The bore in the drawing is many times wider than the millimetre scale beside it, or a 0.8 mm tube would be a hairline.

The equations, in full

Each particle can hold up to n hydrogen bonds, so there are n/2 bonds per particle (every bond is shared by two). A bond costs ε = 1 arbitrary unit to break, and the broken fraction follows a two-state Boltzmann law, x(T) = g / (g + eε/T) with g = 30, where T is the mean motion energy per particle.
Every unit of energy you add has to end up in one of two places: e = T + (n/2) ε x(T), solved for T. The left box has n = 0, so all of its energy has nowhere to go but T. Nothing here is integrated over time — the numbers are the exact solution, and the animation just draws it.

Read this before you convert anything

Every number on this panel is dimensionless. Arbitrary units, per particle, comparing this simulation with itself. Do not translate them into joules, degrees, or J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, and do not read a ratio here as a ratio for real substances. A simulation measures energy per particle, and water’s famous heat capacity is a per gram fact — those are not the same comparison, and they do not even point the same way. The footer below explains why.

Also simplified: the particles are hard discs in two dimensions, bonds are all-or-nothing rather than stretched and bent, and rotation and vibration inside a particle are ignored entirely. Real water stores energy in all of those as well.

What this is for

Cohesion and adhesion are not one stickiness. Reading them as a single property is a fair first move — both are attractions between the same kinds of molecules. But they have different partners — water to water, water to glass — and in a capillary tube they pull against each other. Drop the adhesion slider on its own, touching nothing else, and the meniscus turns over from a smile to a frown and the liquid sinks below the level in the dish. That is the entire difference between water in a glass tube and mercury in a glass tube. Mercury is not less sticky in general; it is enormously more cohesive and barely adheres to glass at all.

What to notice. The whole of panel 1 reduces to one line: h = (2 × adhesion − cohesion) / (ρ g r). Adhesion is on the top with a plus sign and cohesion is on the top with a minus sign — they are genuinely opposed — and the radius is underneath, which is why a narrower tube climbs higher. At 0.80 mm water rises 33.2 mm; at 1.60 mm, exactly half as far, 16.6 mm. That is not a small tube holding less water, it is a small tube holding a taller column, and the reason is that the pull acts around the circumference (which scales with r) while the weight it lifts is a volume (which scales with r²).

Capillary rise needs cohesion too. Attributing the climb entirely to adhesion is reasonable — adhesion is the attraction that touches the glass — but it is only half the mechanism. Set adhesion to its maximum and then drag cohesion down. The liquid does not climb higher — it climbs less. Once the liquid already wets the glass completely (θ = 0°), extra adhesion buys nothing more, and from there it is surface tension — cohesion — that actually holds the weight of the column up. Adhesion grips the wall; cohesion drags the rest of the liquid along behind it. That is precisely the cohesion–tension story of water moving up a hundred metres of xylem: adhesion to the vessel walls and cohesion within the water column, both, or the column breaks.

Adding energy is not the same as speeding particles up. The two normally travel together, so equating them costs nothing most of the time — until there are bonds to break. Panel 2 puts the same energy into two boxes of identical particles and the bonded box ends up moving slower — at the default settings, 64% of the energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds and only 36% into motion, so the particles end up moving at 60% of the speed of the ones next door. Temperature only measures the motion part. Energy spent pulling bonds apart is real, it is stored, and you get it all back when the bonds re-form — but while it is in the bonds, the thermometer cannot see it. That is why a sweating body cools, why coastal towns have milder summers than inland ones, and why a lake takes all autumn to give up its summer heat.

And the trap in panel 2, stated plainly. Water’s celebrated specific heat capacity, 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, is a per gram figure, and a large part of why it wins is simply that a water molecule is unusually small: 18 g mol⁻¹, so a gram of water is an awful lot of molecules. Measure per mole instead — which is what a particle simulation measures, one particle at a time — and water’s molar heat capacity of 75.3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ is among the lowest of the common liquids: benzene is 136, ethanol 112, hexane 196. Water does not beat those molecules particle for particle. It beats them gram for gram. So the boxes on panel 2 are deliberately labelled in arbitrary units and compared only with each other: they show you the mechanism — energy diverted into bonds instead of into motion — and they are not a measurement of water. If you ever see this simulation’s ratio quoted as “water holds four times the heat”, that is the per-gram fact being smuggled into a per-particle picture, and it is wrong.

Why this is on a screen and not on the bench. Surface tension you should meet with a dropper, a penny and a paperclip — a real floating paperclip beats any simulation ever written. What a bench cannot do is hand you a knob for adhesion alone, or let you watch energy divide itself between bonds and motion. Those two are the only reasons this page exists.

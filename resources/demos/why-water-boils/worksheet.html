1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. The point is to have a number on paper that the demonstration will later confirm or contradict.

Prediction

Three of the group 16 hydrides boil at these normal (1 atm) boiling points:

H2S, 34.08 g/mol, −60.3 °C  ·  H2Se, 80.99 g/mol, −41.3 °C  ·  H2Te, 129.62 g/mol, −2.2 °C

Using only those three, predict the boiling point of H2O (18.02 g/mol). Give a number in °C.

Write one sentence saying how you got it.

Water actually boils at 100.0 °C. Subtract your prediction from that. How large is the disagreement, in °C?

2Setting up the control

Group 14 is the control series: nothing in it can hydrogen bond, so only London dispersion is acting. Establish what a well-behaved series looks like before you go looking for a broken one.

  1. Open the demonstration and press Control only. Only the group 14 box should be ticked, and Show extrapolation lines should stay ticked.
  2. Under Inspect a group, the tab 14 should be selected. Read the four boxes: Lightest, Actual b.p., Trend says, Gap.
  3. Below the dividing line, read Trend through the 3 heavy members and Fit quality (R², heavy members).
  4. Record all six values in the table.

The dashed line is an extrapolation, not a measurement. Nobody has ever measured water boiling at −73 °C, because water always has hydrogen bonds. Different reasonable fits put that estimate anywhere from about −70 to −100 °C. What survives all of them is the size of the anomaly: hundreds of degrees, not tens.

4Working backwards, and where the shortcut breaks

A question rarely hands you a fitted line. It hands you two data points and expects you to build one.

  1. Select tab 16 and record the demonstration's Trend says value for H2O together with its Trend through the 3 heavy members slope.
  2. On paper, build your own two-point line through H2S (34.08 g/mol, −60.3 °C) and H2Te (129.62 g/mol, −2.2 °C): find the slope, then use it to predict the boiling point at 18.02 g/mol.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page, or look away from it. Show your working. Then reopen it and check yourself — and where you were wrong, write down what went wrong.

1. Rank CH4, PH3 and NH3 from lowest to highest normal boiling point. Their molar masses are 16.04, 34.00 and 17.03 g/mol. Justify the order using intermolecular forces, not molar mass alone.

2. HCl (36.46 g/mol) boils at −85.1 °C and HI (127.91 g/mol) boils at −35.4 °C. Build the line through those two points and use it to predict the boiling point of HF at 20.01 g/mol. HF actually boils at 19.5 °C. Report the predicted value and the size of the anomaly, each to one decimal place.

3. Fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen, and HF has a larger molar mass than H2O, yet HF boils at 19.5 °C and water at 100.0 °C. Explain the difference. Your answer should refer to how many hydrogen bonds an average molecule of each can form, and to the number of hydrogens and lone pairs available.

4. CH4 also misses the line fitted to its own group, by about −37 °C. Explain why that miss is not evidence of hydrogen bonding in CH4, using both the sign and the size of the miss compared with water's.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The demonstration carries more than this worksheet uses.

  • Untick Show extrapolation lines with all four series on. Using only the shapes of the four measured curves, decide which groups are anomalous. Then tick the box again and see whether you were reading the raw data correctly.
  • The tooltip and the data table both give boiling points in kelvin as well as °C. Refit group 16 in K per g/mol. Which changes, the slope or the gap? Explain why.
  • The data table has a Period column. Sketch the group 16 boiling points against period number instead of molar mass and extrapolate back to period 2. Does water's anomaly survive the change of x-axis, and roughly how large is it now?
  • Compare Fit quality (R², heavy members) across all four tabs. Which series is least linear, and does the ranking of R² have anything to do with the ranking of the gaps?

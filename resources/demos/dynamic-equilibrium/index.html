Honest limits

This is the cleanest possible case. A ⇌ B is one particle in, one particle out, and both directions happen in a single step, so K = kf/kr exactly. For a real reaction like N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, the coefficients become exponents in K and the rate laws are not that simple. What does carry over to every reaction: at equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal, and the amounts usually are not.

What this is for

The misconception: "the reaction has reached equilibrium" invites two readings. Either the reaction has stopped, or reactant and product must now be present in equal amounts. Both readings are reasonable — nothing in the word itself rules them out — and both fail for the same reason: equilibrium is a statement about rates, not about amounts.

What to notice: with the default settings, the box settles at about 100 A and 300 B — nowhere near equal. But the two conversion counters both sit near 6 conversions per step. Six A particles become B and six B particles become A during the same step, so the totals do not move even though roughly a dozen molecules changed identity. That is what "dynamic" means. Look at the box: particles are still flashing as they convert, forever.

Why the amounts are unequal: A flips three times as eagerly as B flips back (kf = 0.060, kr = 0.020). The only way for a lazy reverse reaction to keep up with an eager forward one is to have far more B molecules doing it. Equal rates, unequal amounts — and the ratio it settles at is K = kf/kr = 3.

The second tab is the proof: start from pure reactant and start from pure product. The concentration curves come from opposite directions and land on the same two heights, so both runs report the same K. K belongs to the reaction, not to where you started it. Only the k sliders can change K — and in the lab, only temperature changes their ratio.

Where the model stops being true: the box holds 400 particles, so the counts jitter by a few percent even at equilibrium; a real beaker holds around 1021 molecules and the jitter is invisible. Particle positions here are decoration — this model gives every particle the same chance of flipping regardless of where it is or what it bumps into.

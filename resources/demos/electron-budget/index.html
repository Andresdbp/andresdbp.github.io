What this is for

The misconception: students draw a Lewis structure by walking around the molecule handing every atom an octet, and only count electrons at the end — if they count at all. Done that way, electrons get invented or dropped without anyone noticing. The truth is the opposite order: the moment you write the formula, the total is already fixed. Add up the valence electrons, add one more for every negative charge, subtract one for every positive charge, and that number never changes again. Drawing is spending, not creating.

What to notice: try CO₂ by giving every atom single bonds and full lone pairs. You run out of budget with carbon still short. The fix is never "add a pair" — it is to move a pair you already own off an oxygen and spend it as a second bond. That is the whole reason double and triple bonds exist. Then try NO₃⁻ and NH₄⁺ and watch the budget change by exactly one electron with the charge.

Where the octet rule stops: it is a pattern, not a law. BF₃ leaves boron with 6 electrons and that is the accepted answer. PCl₅ and SF₆ put 10 and 12 around the central atom, which period-3 atoms can do and period-2 atoms (C, N, O, F) never can. NO has 11 electrons — an odd number, so one electron simply cannot be paired. And O₂ is a warning about the model itself: this drawing says all its electrons are paired, but liquid oxygen sticks to a magnet, so two of them are not. Lewis structures are excellent bookkeeping and an imperfect picture of reality.

Honest limits

What is simplified. The outside is treated as a large bath — a cell sitting in a dish of buffer — so its concentration stays exactly where you put the slider and only the cell interior moves. The cell is one well-mixed compartment of 1 pL, which is why 1 fmol of solute inside is exactly 1 mM. All three solutes share one 0–200 mM scale so the routes can be compared side by side; real extracellular values are about 145 mM Na+, 5 mM glucose and 0.1 mM O2. Water movement, membrane voltage and cell volume are held fixed, so nothing here swells or shrinks and no electrical gradient builds — a real Na+/K+-ATPase is electrogenic and exchanges 3 Na+ for 2 K+, which this single-solute pump does not model. Rate constants are chosen so the changes happen on a watchable timescale, not measured from a real cell.

The pump has a real thermodynamic limit that is not drawn here. In this model the pump weakens only because it runs out of Na+ to grab or out of ATP to spend. A real pump also stalls when the gradient gets steep enough that one ATP no longer pays for the move. That is why the gradient here settles where pumping balances leaking, rather than at a hard ceiling.

What this is for

The misconception: students see the word facilitated, see a protein in the diagram, and conclude the cell is paying for it. The reasoning is usually “a protein is doing work, work costs energy, so this must be active transport.” It sounds airtight and it is wrong. Facilitated diffusion is passive. The protein is a doorway, not a hand in the back.

Run the test that settles it. Press Cut ATP supply and keep your eye on all three lanes. The O2 lane does not flinch. The glucose lane does not flinch either — the carrier keeps ferrying glucose at exactly the same rate, and the ATP-test table shows the same number in both columns. Only the Na+ pump stops, and once it does, the gradient it was holding drains away: [Na+] inside climbs from about 9 mM back up to 145 mM, until inside and outside are identical and there is no gradient left. That is the whole distinction, in one button.

The second tell is the shape of the graph. Simple diffusion has no protein, so there is nothing to run out of: double the concentration outside and you double the flux, every time, all the way up the axis — a straight line. Facilitated diffusion has a fixed number of carriers, and once they are all busy, extra glucose just waits its turn. The curve bends over and flattens against a ceiling. On the chart, simple diffusion is the losing route below 65 mM and the winning route above it, and the crossover happens because one curve keeps climbing and the other cannot. That bend is the evidence a protein is involved. It has nothing to do with energy — a straight line versus a hyperbola tells you there is a protein; it does not tell you anything about ATP.

Try to cheat with the carrier. Set glucose to 20 mM outside and 120 mM inside. The carrier is right there, fully functional — and it runs backwards, dumping glucose out of the cell at 5.16 fmol s⁻¹. Add carriers and it goes backwards faster. There is no setting of any slider on this page that makes the carrier move glucose uphill, because the flux expression has ([out] − [in]) in the numerator: when that is negative, so is the flux. A passive protein cannot build a gradient. It can only help one collapse.

And do not conclude that equilibrium means stillness. Press Equalise all sides and watch the O2 and glucose lanes: the net flux reads 0.00, and molecules are still streaming across in both directions at about 10 and 3.7 fmol s⁻¹. Nothing stopped. The two opposing streams just got the same size. “Equilibrium” is a statement about the difference between two rates, never about either rate being zero.

So what is active transport actually for? Look at what the Na+ lane can do that neither other lane can: it holds 145 mM outside against 9 mM inside — a sixteen-fold difference — indefinitely, while a leak channel sits wide open beside it draining it the whole time. That is the thing ATP buys. Not motion, and not a protein. A gradient that will not go away on its own. Every gradient in your body that does useful work — the one that fires your neurons, the one that pulls glucose into your gut lining, the one that makes your ATP in the first place — is being held up by something spending energy against a leak, exactly like this.

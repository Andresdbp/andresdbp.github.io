Where this model stops being true. It assumes the reaction runs to completion with no side reactions, so the yield shown is the theoretical ceiling, not a prediction. Real ammonia synthesis is an equilibrium: one pass through an industrial reactor at about 450 °C and 200 atm converts only roughly 15% of the feed, and the rest is recycled. Percent yield compares a measured mass to the ceiling calculated here.

What this is for

The misconception: students decide which reactant runs out by asking “which do I have less of?” — either in grams, or, after they learn to convert, in moles. Both shortcuts are right often enough to survive several weeks of homework, which is exactly what makes them dangerous. Grams are the wrong currency because a gram of H2 contains almost fourteen times as many molecules as a gram of N2. Moles are the right currency but still the wrong question, because the equation does not consume the two reactants one for one — it eats three H2 for every one N2.

What to notice: on the Grams mislead preset you have only 5.0 g of H2 against 20.0 g of N2, yet nitrogen is what runs out and 0.68 g of hydrogen is still sitting in the flask at the end. On Fewer moles misleads you have far fewer moles of N2 (1.785) than of H2 (4.960), and nitrogen still is not the limiting reactant — dividing by the coefficients flips it, 1.785 against 1.653. The only comparison that survives every preset is moles divided by the coefficient from the balanced equation. Everything else is a coincidence you are one problem away from being burned by.

Check the bookkeeping: the leftover mass is never guessed. It is what remains after the limiting reactant is fully consumed, and the mass line under the result always closes — grams in equals grams out, because atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

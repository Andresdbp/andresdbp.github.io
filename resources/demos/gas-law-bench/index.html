What this is for

The misconception, part one: Boyle, Charles, Gay-Lussac and Avogadro arrive with four names and four formulas, so it is reasonable to file them as four unrelated laws — until a problem changes three things at once and none of the four fits. They are not four laws. There is one equation, PV = nRT, and each "law" is that equation with two of its four variables pinned down. Switch between the four preset buttons without touching a slider: the gas never changes, only the camera angle does. The readout PV/(nT) sits on 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) the entire time. That is the whole subject in one number.

The misconception, part two: putting Celsius into a gas-law ratio looks harmless — degrees are degrees, and the arithmetic runs without complaining. Here is the size of the damage. Set 1.000 mol at 1.000 atm and 298.15 K (25.00 °C): the volume is 24.47 L. Warm it to 373.15 K (100.00 °C) and the true volume is 30.62 L. Do it "in Celsius" instead — 24.47 L × (100/25) — and you get 97.9 L, more than three times too big. Start from 0 °C and the Celsius version divides by zero and returns nothing at all.

What to notice: put V on the vertical axis, T on the horizontal, and set the axis to °C. The line is straight, but it does not go through the origin — at 0 °C the gas still has 22.41 L of volume, because 0 °C is just the freezing point of water and has nothing to do with gases. Follow the line left. It reaches V = 0 at −273.15 °C, and everything past that point is shaded, dashed, and negative: a gas with negative volume, which is not a thing. Now flip the axis to kelvin. Same line, same gas, but now it lands on the origin, because 0 K is −273.15 °C. The kelvin scale was not invented to annoy you; the gases picked the zero and Kelvin just moved the label there.

The rule underneath it: a ratio only means something when zero on the scale means zero of the thing being measured. 2 L really is twice 1 L, and 2 atm really is twice 1 atm, so V and P ratios work. 20 °C is not twice 10 °C in any physical sense — it is 293.15 K against 283.15 K, a 3.5% difference, not a doubling. Notice that Boyle's law is the one preset with no temperature in it at all. That is why a Celsius habit can survive a whole run of problems without being caught, and then fail badly the first time temperature enters the ratio.

The honest caveat: PV = nRT is a model, not a law of nature. It assumes gas particles have no volume of their own and no attraction for each other, which is false for every real gas. At 0 °C and 1 atm the model predicts 22.41 L per mole; helium measures about 22.43 L, nitrogen about 22.40 L, carbon dioxide about 22.26 L and ammonia about 22.08 L. Those errors are under 1.5% because the gas is dilute. Squeeze the molar volume down — the readout panel flags it — or cool the gas toward its boiling point, and the errors grow to tens of percent. This bench will happily draw you a smooth curve through 80 K; a real sample of carbon dioxide would have been a solid block since 195 K, and the equation would not warn you.

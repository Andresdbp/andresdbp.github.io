The one thing to do

Why they diverge at all
3.0 × 10⁻³

Extra divergence per generation because the two habitats favour different traits. Set it to zero and the populations still drift apart — just more slowly, at 1/(2N) = 1.0 × 10⁻² per generation from genetic drift alone.

Divergence per generation = (drift + selection) × (1 − D) − 2m × D, where D is divergence and m is the migration rate. The first term builds difference, the second erases it.

Honest limits

The isolation index is invented for this page. Real biologists do not have a single number called "reproductive isolation" that ticks up from 0 to 1. They measure separate things: whether the two forms will court each other, whether the eggs get fertilised, whether the hybrids survive, whether the hybrids are fertile. This index rolls all of that into one dimensionless 0–1 number so you can watch the shape of the change. Treat the shape as the lesson and the digits as scenery.

This is allopatric speciation only — populations split by a physical barrier (geography). It is the easiest case and the one AP Biology asks about first. Speciation also happens without a barrier (sympatric speciation: polyploidy in plants, host-race shifts in insects, assortative mating in one lake). Nothing on this page models that, and the fact that you have to raise a barrier here does not mean a barrier is required in nature.

What is simplified. Divergence D is a single number standing in for a whole genome, closest in spirit to FST. Each population has an effective size of N = 50 and they are the same size; migration is symmetric. Drift is put in twice on purpose — once as the deterministic pressure 1/(2N) per generation, which is the expected behaviour, and once as a small random wobble so runs are not identical. Hybrid fitness falls as exp(−8D⁵), a "snowball": incompatible gene pairs accumulate faster than the genes themselves, so hybrids stay healthy for a long time and then fail quickly. Real snowballs are messier and mutation order matters. There is no mutation input, no population growth, no ecology, and no reinforcement: this page never lets selection against bad hybrids push mate choice further apart, which in nature can finish the job.

What this is for

The misconception: students picture speciation as a moment. One population, then a wall goes up, then — at some particular generation — ping, two species. They expect there to be a birthday. Textbook definitions encourage it: "a species is a group that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring" sounds like a yes/no test, so speciation sounds like the instant the answer flips from yes to no.

There is no such instant. Press Split 60 gen and watch. The barrier goes up, divergence climbs to about 0.60, isolation reaches roughly 0.6, and hybrids are already only about half as fit as their parents. Then the barrier drops and the two populations pour back into each other. Within roughly ten generations of contact the divergence curve is flat on the floor: they are one population again, and every trace of the split is gone. Now press Split 260 gen. Exactly the same thing happens for the first 60 generations. The barrier comes down 200 generations later, the populations meet — and nothing fuses. They stay two.

The interesting run is the one in between. Press Split 140 gen. When that barrier drops, the isolation index reads about 0.99 and hybrid fitness is about 0.03 — hybrids are born, and almost all of them die or fail to breed. Whether the populations re-fuse or stay apart now hangs on a hair, and the small random wobble in drift genuinely decides it — run that same preset eight times and it lands on "one species" about five times and "two species" about three. Run it twice yourself. That knife-edge is the answer to the question. If speciation were an event, there would be a generation where the outcome switched cleanly from "one species" to "two". Instead there is a wide, slow, ambiguous band where the honest answer is partly.

Watch the last fraction of a percent do the work. Here is the number that surprises people. Stop a run at 100 generations of separation: isolation is already about 0.96 — 96% of gene flow blocked, hybrid fitness down to about 0.10, nine hybrids in ten failing. Lower the barrier and they fuse anyway, in about twenty generations. Why? Because 4% of ten potential migrants is still roughly 0.4 migrants per generation landing their genes, and at that divergence the trickle erases difference faster than drift and selection can build it. Isolation has to climb past about 0.994 — under 0.06 migrants a generation — before contact stops undoing the split. "Ninety-six percent isolated" is not "nearly a species"; it is barely started. That is exactly why the middle of the process is so long and so hard to call.

And gene flow is brutally strong. Set the leak slider to 1.00, raise the barrier, and wait as long as you like. Divergence stalls near 0.25 and never moves again. One animal in fifty crossing once a generation cancels drift and most of the selection at once. Turn the leak down to 0.25 and the same barrier now produces a new species. This is why "the barrier" in a speciation story has to be a real barrier: a mountain range that is almost impassable is, genetically, not a barrier at all.

So where do the two species come from? Nowhere in particular. Divergence is a number that goes up while populations are apart and down while they are together, and reproductive isolation is a consequence of that number. When we say "these are two species now", we are drawing a line on a smooth curve because our language needs nouns. The curve does not have a step in it. That is why biologists argue about whether particular pairs — polar and brown bears, coyotes and wolves, herring gulls around the Arctic — are one species or two. They are not being sloppy. They are being asked to name a point on a ramp.

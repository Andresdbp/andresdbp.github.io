The enzyme is a catalyst. It is not used up, not consumed, and not converted into product. One enzyme molecule here turns over 1000 substrate molecules every minute and is unchanged at the end of each one. Nothing in this model depletes it.

Honest limits

Denaturation here is reversible. The folded fraction is computed as a two-state equilibrium, so cooling the slider back down really does restore the rate — drag temperature to 55 °C and back to 37 °C and watch. That is Anfinsen’s result: for a small single-domain protein the sequence alone contains the folding information, and it can refold. In a real tube it usually does not, because unfolded chains stick to each other and aggregate, and aggregation is irreversible. That is a concentration problem, not a rule of physics.

Denaturation does not cut the chain. Heat and extreme pH break the weak interactions that hold the three-dimensional shape — hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic packing — and can break disulfide bridges. The peptide bonds are untouched. A denatured enzyme still has its full primary sequence in order; it has simply lost the shape that made the active site fit the substrate.

What is simplified. Rates are initial rates (v0): no product has accumulated and the reverse reaction is ignored. Steady-state Michaelis–Menten is assumed, which needs [S] ≫ [E]total — true everywhere on these sliders. The inhibitors are modelled as pure types: competitive changes only Km, noncompetitive only Vmax. Real inhibitors are usually mixed, sitting somewhere between the two, and a third class (uncompetitive, which binds only the ES complex and lowers both) is not included here. Temperature and pH are modelled as acting on Vmax alone; in a real enzyme they nudge Km a little too. The enzyme is a generic one-substrate, one-product catalyst, so the numbers are round rather than measured.

What this is for

Where the topic usually stops: a competitive inhibitor sits in the active site, a noncompetitive inhibitor sits somewhere else. Both statements are true, and binding site is the cause — but stopping there makes the whole topic a question of where the molecule parks. The question actually asked on the exam, and the only one a cell cares about, is the consequence: can you beat the inhibitor by adding more substrate? For one of these two the answer is yes, completely. For the other it is no, not ever, no matter how much you add.

What to notice. Press Competitive, leave [S] at 2.00 mM, and the rate collapses from 50.0 to 14.3 µmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹. Now press Flood with substrate. The rate climbs to 97.7 — back on top of the uninhibited curve. Nothing was removed and no inhibitor left; substrate simply outnumbered it at the door. The Vmax tile never twitched, the whole time. What moved was Km: 2.00 mM → 12.0 mM, because you now need six times as much substrate to reach the same half-maximal rate. Now press Noncompetitive and do exactly the same thing. The rate at 2.00 mM is 8.33; flood it to 500 mM and you get 16.6, and that is where it stops forever. This time Km sat at 2.00 mM without moving and Vmax fell from 100 to 16.7. Substrate bought you nothing.

Why. A competitive inhibitor and the substrate are queueing for the same seat, so it is a numbers game: Km,app = Km(1 + [I]/Ki) grows without limit, but Vmax cannot change, because Vmax is the rate when every enzyme molecule is already holding substrate — and at high enough [S] you can always get there. A noncompetitive inhibitor binds its own site whether or not substrate is bound, so substrate never competes with it at all. It simply takes enzyme molecules out of service: Vmax,app = Vmax/(1 + [I]/Ki), with Km untouched, because the enzymes that are still working bind substrate exactly as well as they always did. Test that claim on this page: switch the noncompetitive inhibitor off and slide [E]total from 100 nM down to about 17 nM instead. You get the same curve. Noncompetitive inhibition is having fewer working enzymes.

The double-reciprocal view says it in one picture. 1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax, so the y-intercept is 1/Vmax and the x-intercept is −1/Km. Competitive: both lines cross the vertical axis at the same 0.0100, because Vmax did not move. Noncompetitive: both lines cross the horizontal axis at the same −0.500, because Km did not move. That is the tell, and it is not a memorised picture — it is the same two facts, drawn.

One more thing the graphs will teach you. The temperature curve is not a bell. It rises gently and roughly exponentially over forty degrees, then falls off a cliff in about ten, because two different things are happening: warmer molecules collide harder and the protein starts losing its fold. Half-maximum activity sits at 27.5 °C on the way up and 46.2 °C on the way down — the peak at 40.5 °C is nowhere near the middle. And the collapse on the right is not the chain being cut. Denaturation breaks the weak interactions holding the shape; the peptide bonds, and the amino acid sequence, survive intact.

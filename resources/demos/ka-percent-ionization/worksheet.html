3What dilution actually changes

One control moves here: concentration. Ka stays where it is, and you can watch the Ka tile to confirm that.

  2. Press Dilute ×10 once and record the next row. Repeat until you reach 1.00 × 10−5 M.

a. In the top few rows, each ten-fold dilution multiplies the percent ionized by roughly the same factor. What is it, to two significant figures, and what is √10?

b. That factor shrinks in the bottom rows. Percent ionized there is no longer small, so one approximation behind the √10 pattern has stopped holding. Which one, and why does it fail once a large fraction of the acid has ionized?

c. By how much does the pH change per ten-fold dilution in the top rows? A strong acid diluted ten-fold changes pH by exactly 1.00 unit. Account for the difference.

d. State the rule in your own words: on dilution, what happens to percent ionization, to pH, to [H3O+], and to Ka?

e. Return to your part 1 prediction. Which of the three circles were borne out? For any that were not, name the specific assumption behind it.

A larger fraction of a smaller amount can still be less acid. Both columns rose at once and neither reading is wrong — check your [H3O+] column and confirm it fell the whole way down.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Then reopen it, set each solution up, and check — and where you disagree, find the line where it went wrong. All values are for 25 °C.

1. Formic acid, HCOOH, has Ka = 1.8 × 10−4. A solution is 0.0100 M. Find [H3O+], pH and percent ionization by solving the equilibrium exactly — the quadratic, not the approximation. Then compute what the “x is small” approximation would have given, and state the percentage by which it misses [H3O+].

2. Hypochlorous acid, HClO, has Ka = 3.0 × 10−8. A solution is 0.100 M. Find [H3O+], pH and percent ionization. You may assume x is small here — but finish by showing the check that justifies the assumption.

3. Two flasks each contain 0.100 mol of acid per litre: one HF (Ka = 6.8 × 10−4), one HCl. Find the pH of each and the ratio of their hydronium concentrations. Then explain, in terms of what Ka measures, why identical amounts of acid give pH values more than a unit apart.

4. At 1.00 × 10−5 M, the “x is small” approximation reports acetic acid as 134.2% ionized. Without calculating the exact answer, explain which assumption produced a number above 100%, why every genuine percent ionization must lie below it, and whether the true value is larger or smaller than 134.2%. State also what happens to Ka between 0.100 M and 1.00 × 10−5 M.

6On your own

Nothing here is collected. The demonstration holds more than this worksheet used.

  • Select HClO (pKa 7.52) and dilute it all the way to 1.00 × 10−5 M. It never comes near 50% ionized. Using pH = pKa at 50%, and the fact that the pH curve leans on the pH 7 line without crossing it, work out why that ceiling exists for any acid with pKa above 7.
  • Leave the presets behind and drag the Ka slider itself. Set it to its maximum, 1.0 × 10−3, at 0.100 M and see how ionized the strongest acid the slider allows actually gets. Then dilute until it reaches 50% and note the concentration.
  • Select HCN, press Watch it dilute, and keep your eye on the H3O+ from water tile. Find roughly where water starts supplying more than half of the hydronium, and decide what still makes it reasonable to call that flask an acid solution.
  • Read the Honest limits panel. Name one thing the model leaves out that a real 1 M flask would show, and say which direction it would push the numbers.

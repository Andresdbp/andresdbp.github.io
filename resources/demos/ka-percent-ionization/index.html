Honest limits

25 °C throughout. Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ only at that temperature, and every pH here depends on it. The solution is found from the full charge balance [H3O+] = [A−] + [OH−] together with [A−] = KaC / (Ka + [H3O+]), so water’s own ionization is included at every concentration — that is why the pH curve flattens under pH 7 instead of crossing it.

What is simplified: concentrations are used in place of activities, so Ka is treated as genuinely constant. In a real flask Ka drifts a little with ionic strength, and at 1 M the drift is not tiny. The acid is monoprotic, nothing else is dissolved, and the acid is assumed fully soluble. Below about 10⁻⁶ M the “solution” is mostly just water — the model is still right, but the chemistry stops being about your acid, which is what the H₃O⁺ from water readout is there to tell you.

What this is for

Two words that are not on the same scale: weak and dilute turn up in the same sentences often enough to start sounding like synonyms, and so do strong and concentrated. They answer completely different questions. Concentrated or dilute is about how much acid you put in the flask — you choose it, and it is the concentration slider here. Weak or strong is about what fraction of that acid actually gives its proton away — the acid decides that, through Ka, and you cannot change it by adding water.

What to notice: set acetic acid at 0.100 M. It is 1.33% ionized at pH 2.88. Now press “Watch it dilute” and follow both curves at once. At 1.00 × 10⁻⁵ M the same acid is 71.6% ionized — fifty times the fraction — and the pH has risen to 5.15. Percent ionization went up and the solution got less acidic, at the same time, from the same action. Read quickly, that looks like a fault in the model rather than a result. It is a result. A far larger share of a far smaller amount of acid is still less acid: 71.6% of 10⁻⁵ M is a hundred times less hydronium than 1.33% of 0.100 M.

Why it happens — Le Chatelier, applied to the ionization itself. HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−. Count the dissolved particles: one on the left, two on the right (water is the solvent, so it does not count). Adding water spreads everything out, and the equilibrium answers by shifting toward the side that makes more dissolved particles — the ionized side. Dilution is a stress, and the system relieves it by ionizing further. Ka never moved through any of this; watch the Ka tile sit still while the percent climbs.

And the side-by-side settles the other half. Pick HF at 1 M: it is 2.57% ionized, pH 1.59, while a strong acid at the very same 1 M is 100% ionized at pH 0.00. Same concentration, different extent — that is the whole meaning of the word weak. Then dilute both to 10⁻⁵ M: HF reaches 98.6% ionized and its pH, 5.01, sits within 0.01 of the strong acid’s. HF has not become a strong acid. Its Ka is exactly the 6.8 × 10⁻⁴ it always was. Percent ionization is a property of this solution; Ka is a property of the acid, and only one of them is allowed to move.

Where the model would have lied to you: the usual classroom shortcut assumes x is small and gives [H3O+] = √(KaC). At acetic acid, 10⁻⁵ M, that shortcut reports 134.2% ionization — more acid ionized than exists. It fails hardest exactly where this demonstration lives, so nothing here uses it. Water matters too: try HCN at 10⁻⁵ M, where 61.8% of the hydronium comes from water, not from the acid. Drop the water term and the arithmetic will hand you a pH above 7 for an acid, which is impossible — a real acid solution can creep toward pH 7 from below and never reach it.

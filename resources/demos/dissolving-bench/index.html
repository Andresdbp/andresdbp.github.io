Where the model simplifies. Concentrations use measured solution densities at 25 °C, so the volume really does change as solute dissolves — watch the level. Conductivity is interpolated from measured NaCl data at 25 °C. “Dissolved particles” assumes every formula unit separates completely, which is exactly true only in dilute solution. The number of particles drawn on screen grows with the amount added but is schematic — read the numbers, not the dots. Real sugar water in a lab often gives a faint glow, because tap water and dissolved CO2 bring their own ions; the sucrose itself contributes none.

What this is for

The misconception: students say the salt “disappeared” or “melted”, and they picture NaCl(aq) as billions of tiny, still-intact NaCl units drifting around in the water. That picture feels harmless now. It is the thing that breaks every complete-ionic and net-ionic equation later in the year, because those equations only work if you know that the salt is already in pieces before any reaction starts.

What to notice: the bulb. Salt water and sugar water look identical — both are clear and colourless, both taste of something, both went in as a white solid. But only one of them lights the lamp. A bulb needs charge to move through the liquid, and charge can only move if separate charged particles are free to travel. Sugar water is dark because a sucrose molecule went in whole and stayed whole: C12H22O11(aq) is one neutral particle, so there is nothing to carry current. Salt water glows because Na+ and Cl− left the crystal separately and never got back together.

Also worth noticing: dissolving is not melting. Nothing here was heated, and the water stayed liquid the whole time. And in the close-up, look at which end of the water molecule faces which ion — the oxygen end turns toward Na+, the hydrogen ends turn toward Cl−. Opposites attract, and water has both ends available. That single picture is also the answer to most later intermolecular-force questions.

Hexane is not a story about weak attraction. Hexane molecules attract each other perfectly well, and water attracts water very strongly. The problem is that they attract in incompatible ways. Water molecules hold onto each other by hydrogen bonds, and hexane cannot make a hydrogen bond at all. To fit hexane in, the surrounding water has to freeze into a more ordered cage so it can keep bonding to itself around the intruder, and hexane offers nothing back to repay that cost. So the mixture settles into two layers. “Like dissolves like” is a statement about matching polarity, not about which substance is stronger.

Where the model stops being true: the drawing is flat, and a real hydration shell is three-dimensional — about six water molecules around Na+, swapping partners billions of times a second rather than sitting still. Sizes on screen use real ionic and molecular radii (Na+ 102 pm, Cl− 181 pm, water about 152 pm), but the counts do not.

Every model keeps all the original 15N — at generation 0 it is 2−0 of the total DNA no matter which model is true. The models disagree only about how that 15N is packaged, and that is what a density gradient can see.

Where the model simplifies

Densities use the measured values for E. coli DNA in caesium chloride: 1.710 g/cm³ for all-14N and 1.724 g/cm³ for all-15N. Everything between is straight-line interpolation on the fraction of 15N. Band widths here are schematic; real bands are broadened by diffusion, and by generation 3 or 4 the faint bands are hard to read on real film. The counts assume every molecule replicates exactly once per generation and that no strand is lost.

What this is for

The misconception: almost every student can say “replication is semiconservative,” and almost none can say what the other two models predicted. That is a memorized conclusion, not an experiment. If you cannot state what conservative and dispersive replication would have put in the tube, you have no idea why Meselson and Stahl mattered — you are just trusting the textbook.

What to notice: step to generation 1 and compare the three tubes. At generation 0 all three models say the same thing — one fully heavy band — so nothing has been tested yet. Conservative dies as soon as growth in 14N starts: it is the only model that still puts DNA at the fully heavy line afterwards, and at generation 1 that band should hold half the DNA. But semiconservative and dispersive give you the identical picture — one band, exactly halfway, at 1.7170 g/cm³. One generation of data cannot choose between them, and a student who says “generation 1 proved replication is semiconservative” is wrong.

Generation 2 is the whole experiment. Semiconservative predicts the DNA splits into two bands: half of it still hybrid at 1.7170 (the two original heavy strands are still intact, one per molecule), half of it fully light at 1.7100. Dispersive predicts one band at 1.7135 — every molecule is a uniform quarter-heavy patchwork, so they all sit at the same density and there is nothing to split. Two bands versus one band: that is the observation, and Meselson and Stahl saw two.

The honest caveat: the dispersive band never stays put. It slides toward the light line generation after generation, and by generation 4 it is only 6% 15N, sitting at 1.7109 — close enough to plain light DNA that real film would struggle to separate them. That is exactly why the argument rests on generation 2, when the dispersive band is still clearly off the light line but the semiconservative prediction has already broken in two.

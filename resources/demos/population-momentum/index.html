How this model works, and what it leaves out

This is a simplified cohort-component projection. Eighteen five-year age groups (0–4 up to 85+) are stepped forward five years at a time. In each step every group ages into the next one and loses a fixed share to death; births are produced only by women in the seven groups from 15–19 to 45–49, spread across those ages in a fixed schedule with a mean age of childbearing of 29.1 years; the newborns become the new 0–4 group.

There is no migration in this model. Nobody enters or leaves the population — every change on screen comes from births and deaths alone. For many real countries migration is the largest single term, so treat these curves as showing what age structure does by itself, not as a forecast for any actual country.

Also simplified: one survival schedule is used for both sexes and it never improves over time, so rising life expectancy is not modelled; the shape of the fertility schedule is fixed, and only its total moves when you drag the slider; the population is treated as one uniform group with no regions, wealth differences, or randomness; and time moves in five-year jumps, so every reading is a snapshot of a projection step rather than a continuous curve. Population is scaled to start at 100 million so that percentages read directly off the numbers — the units are arbitrary and only the ratios matter.

What this is for

The misconception: students hear that replacement fertility is about 2.1 children per woman and conclude that a country hitting 2.1 has stopped growing. It has not. Fertility is a rate per woman, and the population still contains an enormous cohort of children who have not yet become women. Growth does not stop when fertility falls — it stops roughly one human lifetime later, once that bulge has finished passing through the childbearing ages. Two smaller mistakes travel with it: that replacement is exactly 2.0 (it is about 2.1, because some girls die before finishing their own reproductive years and about 105 boys are born for every 100 girls), and that an age pyramid is just a picture of the present. It is not. It is a forecast.

What to notice. Load the page as it opens: an expansive pyramid, built by a fertility of 3.91 children per woman, 34.2% of everyone under age 15, growing at 2.2% per year. Now the fertility slider is already at 2.1 — replacement — from year 0. Fertility is not high any more. Press Play. The population goes from 100.0 million to 159.5 million. It is still climbing at year 40. It is still climbing at year 60. Ninety-five percent of that extra 59.5% arrives in the first 60 years, and growth only drops below a tenth of a percent per year at year 70. Nothing was added to make that happen. No fertility spike, no immigration — migration is switched off entirely. Every one of those 59.5 million extra people was already visible in the pyramid on the first frame, standing in the bottom three rows waiting to grow up.

Why it happens — watch the “Women aged 15–49” tile. Births equal fertility per woman multiplied by the number of women. At year 0 there are 22.6 million women of reproductive age. By year 40 there are 33.7 million, an increase of about half, because the children in the wide base of the pyramid have simply grown up. So even with every woman having only 2.1 children, the country produces far more births than deaths for decades, purely because there are far more potential mothers than there are old people dying. That gap is the momentum. It closes only when the bulge reaches the top of the pyramid and starts dying, which takes the better part of a century.

Push it to the point of absurdity. Drag the new fertility all the way down to 1.6 — far below replacement, a level associated with shrinking countries. The population still grows, to 123.9 million, and keeps growing for 40 years before it turns over. You have to go down near 1.0 child per woman, less than half of replacement, before growth stops within a couple of decades — and even then the population rises to 105.4 million first. Now drag the phase-in slider to 40 years, which is what an actual fertility decline looks like: the stored growth more than doubles, to +154%. Then press Contracting and try the same experiments. On an old pyramid the momentum runs the other way: the population keeps shrinking even when fertility is lifted back to replacement, because the large cohorts are now in the ages that die rather than the ages that give birth.

Where this model stops being true. It has no migration at all, which for many real countries is the biggest term of the three. Survival never improves, so it understates growth wherever life expectancy is still rising. And it assumes fertility changes as a clean step or ramp that applies to every age at once. The lesson that survives all of those simplifications is the structural one: a population is not a single number, it is a set of cohorts of different sizes, and the sizes of those cohorts commit the future decades in advance. That is why demographers can project a country's population to 2070 with far more confidence than they can predict next year's birth rate — most of the people who will be alive in 2070 are already born.

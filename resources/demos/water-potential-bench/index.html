Solute (both sides / the bath)

i is the ionisation constant: how many particles one formula unit becomes in water. Sucrose stays as one molecule. NaCl splits into Na+ and Cl−, so the same molarity does twice the osmotic work. What matters to water is particles per litre, not what they are.

295 K

R = 0.0831 L·bar·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, so RT = 24.51 L·bar·mol⁻¹. A 1.00 M non-ionising solute therefore has Ψs = −24.51 bar. T must be in kelvin, never °C.

Ψp here is not a slider and nobody is pressing on anything. It is the pressure the liquid column itself exerts at the membrane: ρgh, which for water is 0.000981 bar per cm of height. Both potentials are read at the membrane, the only place water actually crosses. Atmospheric pressure pushes down on both open surfaces equally, so it cancels and is taken as zero.

Hypotonic, hypertonic and isotonic are adjectives for the solution, and they only mean anything once you name the cell you are comparing it with. There is no such thing as a hypotonic cell. Drag the bath to 0.35 Osm and this panel says two different words about one beaker.

Where this model stops being true

The U-tube runs on millimolar solutions and a three-metre tube on purpose. A column of water is a feeble source of pressure: 1 cm of it is only 0.000981 bar. To balance a 1.0 M solution (Ψs = −24.5 bar) you would need a column 250 m tall. Real bench U-tubes never reach equilibrium for exactly this reason — they rise a few centimetres and keep creeping. Diluting to millimolar is what lets you actually watch osmosis stop.

What else is simplified. Ψs = −iCRT is the van 't Hoff law, exact only for dilute ideal solutions; real NaCl behaves as i ≈ 1.87, not 2.00, because the ions are not fully independent. Solution density is taken as water's 1.00 g·mL⁻¹ for the ρgh calculation, and the short channel joining the arms is treated as having no volume. The membrane leaks nothing at all, ever — real membranes do leak, and a solute that crosses freely (urea, ethanol) generates no lasting osmotic pull no matter how concentrated it is. The plant cell wall is modelled as a linear spring with elastic modulus ε = 50 bar; the red blood cell is set to burst at 1.45× its normal volume. How fast water moves here is a made-up rate constant. The direction and the endpoint are physics.

What this is for

The first misconception: students compress osmosis into one rule — water moves toward the higher solute concentration — and then quietly assume it keeps going until the two concentrations are equal. The rule is a decent guess for the first instant. The conclusion is wrong, and this page is built to break it. Start the U-tube with 5.0 mM on the left and pure water on the right and press Jump to equilibrium. Water stops crossing when the left arm stands at 197.5 cm and the right at 102.5 cm. Now read the concentrations: 3.80 mM on the left, 0.00 mM on the right. Not equal. Not close to equal. And nothing is going to move them, because the thing water actually responds to is not concentration — it is Ψ, and Ψ is +0.101 bar on both sides.

What is doing the balancing. The left arm still has the more negative Ψs (−0.093 bar against 0.000). What cancels it is Ψp: the left column is 94.9 cm taller, and 94.9 cm of water presses down at the membrane with 0.093 bar. Solute pull on one side, column weight on the other, exactly equal. This is the entire content of Ψ = Ψp + Ψs, and it is why the formula has two terms instead of one. Watch Ψp in the table as the sim runs: nobody set it, no slider controls it, it builds itself out of the water that already moved. Osmosis generates the pressure that stops osmosis.

Which way does water go? From higher Ψ to lower Ψ — from less negative to more negative. Check it against the numbers rather than against your intuition, every time. At the start of that run the right arm sits at Ψ = +0.147 bar and the left at +0.025 bar, so water crosses right to left and the salty side rises. That agrees with the memorised rule. Now do the experiment that breaks it: with the tube resting at 197.5 / 102.5 cm, press Match the concentrations, keep the levels. Solute is stirred into the short arm until both sides read 3.80 mM. The concentration gradient is now exactly zero, so the “more solute” rule has nothing whatsoever to say — and water immediately pours out of the tall arm, because Ψ is +0.101 bar on the left and only +0.007 bar on the right. Twenty-nine millilitres cross back and the tube settles at 168.6 / 131.4 cm, still not level, because the transfer pulls the concentrations apart again (4.45 mM against 2.96 mM). Pressure moved water against no concentration gradient at all. That is what pushes sap up a root at night and what makes a plant guttate.

The second misconception: students say “the cell is hypotonic.” The words describe the solution, and only relative to a named cell — which means one beaker can be two different things at once. Switch to Cells and press The one that splits them (0.350 Osm). The animal cell holds 0.300 Osm, so the bath is hypertonic to it and it shrinks to 0.86 of normal volume. The plant cell holds 0.400 Osm, so the very same bath is hypotonic to it and water moves in until the wall is pushing back at Ψp = 1.03 bar. One solution, two opposite directions of water flow, in the same beaker at the same moment. “Hypertonic” is not a property of the liquid on its own any more than “taller” is a property of one person.

Why the wall changes the ending, and not the mechanism. Both cells obey Ψ = Ψp + Ψs; the difference is that the animal cell's Ψp is stuck at 0. A bare plasma membrane cannot hold pressure, so the cell has no way to raise its own Ψ and push back. Its only route to equilibrium is to take in water until its contents are as dilute as the bath — and in pure water that means infinite volume, so it bursts instead (lysis, here at 1.45× volume). The plant cell has a wall, so as water enters, Ψp climbs and its Ψ rises to meet the bath's. In pure water it stops at Ψp = +8.40 bar, Ψs = −8.40 bar, Ψ = 0 — full turgor, and precisely the “Ψp = −Ψs” case from the formula sheet. Same physics, same equation, one extra structure, and the plant cell lives.

One more number worth chasing. Set the bath to Plant just goes flaccid (0.400 Osm). The plant cell sits at exactly its normal volume with Ψp = 0 — incipient plasmolysis, the knife-edge between turgid and plasmolysed. That is not a curiosity: it is how the Ψs of real plant tissue is measured in a lab. You find the external concentration at which half the cells are just beginning to plasmolyse, and because Ψp = 0 there, Ψs inside = Ψsolution = −iCRT. Push past it and the protoplast pulls away from the wall, which stays exactly where it was — a plasmolysed cell is not a smaller cell, it is a cell with a gap.

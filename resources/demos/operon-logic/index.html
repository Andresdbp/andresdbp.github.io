Honest limits

The inducer is allolactose, not lactose. Lactose that gets into the cell is converted by β-galactosidase into allolactose, and that is the molecule that binds the repressor. This page draws lactose and allolactose as one amber ligand for clarity.

“Off” is never zero. A fully repressed lac operon still transcribes at about 0.5% of maximum here. That leak is not a rounding error — it is the reason the cell owns a few permease and β-galactosidase molecules at all times, which is the only way lactose can get in and be turned into allolactose in the first place. A truly silent operon could never be induced.

What is simplified. The real operon has three operators (O1, O2, O3) and the repressor is a tetramer that loops the DNA between them; here there is one operator. Glucose is modelled only through cAMP and CAP. In a real cell glucose also blocks lactose uptake directly — unphosphorylated EIIAGlc shuts down the lac permease, which is called inducer exclusion. Levels are percentages of the fully induced rate, not molecules per minute; the exact percentages are illustrative, the ordering and the logic are not.

How the number is built

Basal promoter 2.0 units. Each bound activator multiplies: Sp1 ×1.5, HNF-4α ×5, C/EBPα ×6. MeCP2 on the silencer divides by 12. Full activation is 2.0 × 1.5 × 5 × 6 = 90.0 units, and the readout is that raw figure as a percentage of 90.0. Multiplying, not adding, is the point: two activators together give far more than the two of them separately, which is why enhancers behave as combinations rather than as a tally.

What is simplified. The horizontal axis of the map is not to scale; the enhancers really are thousands of base pairs away and their positions here are squashed so they fit. Real enhancers also work in either orientation and from either side of the gene. Chromatin is left out entirely — in a real neuron MeCP2 does not just sit there, it recruits histone deacetylases and the region is packed away, which is a far more durable off switch than the twelve-fold division used here. The factor names are real proteins; the numbers attached to them are illustrative.

What this is for

The misconception: students learn one sentence — “lactose present, lac operon on” — and then get taken apart by the question that actually gets asked. Lactose is only half the input. The operon is an AND gate: it runs hard only when lactose is present and glucose is absent. Set both sugars present on this page and transcription reads 4.94% of maximum, not 100%. The cell is doing the sensible thing: if glucose is on the table, why build the machinery for the second-choice sugar?

Glucose is not a second repressor. This is the other half of the trap, and it is the one that survives into college. Glucose never touches the operator and never touches the repressor. Glucose lowers cAMP. With cAMP low, CAP has no cAMP to bind, so CAP does not sit on its site, so it never bends the DNA and never helps RNA polymerase get started. Removing a helper is not the same event as adding a blocker, and the page is built so you can see the difference: the repressor readout and the CAP readout move independently, and the CAP⁻ strain proves it. Delete CAP entirely and lactose still induces the operon — from 0.010% up to 2.00% — but it can never reach 100%, because the positive control is gone. Notice too that in a CAP⁻ strain the glucose switch stops doing anything at all. That is only possible if glucose was acting through CAP the whole time.

Read the four mutants as logic, not as vocabulary. lacI⁻ has no working repressor, so the operator is always free: the operon is constitutive with respect to lactose — 100% with no lactose at all. Oᶜ has a working repressor that has nowhere to land, and gives the identical four numbers. (They are indistinguishable in one cell. You separate them with a partial diploid: a good lacI gene on an F′ plasmid rescues lacI⁻, because the repressor protein diffuses; it cannot rescue Oᶜ, because a broken operator only ruins the copy of the operon it is physically attached to.) lacIˢ is the mirror image — a repressor that grips the operator but can no longer feel allolactose — so it reads OFF in all four boxes and lactose does nothing. And here is the detail worth catching: lacI⁻ and Oᶜ are still glucose-sensitive. They drop from 100% to 4.94% the moment you add glucose, because losing the repressor does not give you CAP.

Two honest things about “off”. The repressed operon still transcribes at about 0.5%, and it has to. Something has to make the handful of permease molecules that let the first lactose in and the handful of β-galactosidase molecules that turn it into allolactose; a genuinely silent operon could never notice that lactose had arrived. And the inducer is allolactose, an isomer β-galactosidase makes from lactose — not lactose itself.

Then the second tab, which is the whole reason the first one is on the syllabus. Bacteria switch a few genes with a repressor sitting in the way. You do not. Your liver cell and your neuron carry byte-for-byte the same DNA — the same gene, the same two enhancers twelve and six and a half kilobases upstream, the same silencer. The gene reads 100% in the hepatocyte and 0.278% in the neuron, and not one base pair differs between them. What differs is which transcription factor proteins are in the nucleus: HNF-4α and C/EBPα are made in liver and not in neurons, while MeCP2 is stockpiled in neurons at a level no other cell comes close to. Note the third cell too. The muscle cell sits at 3.33%, off simply because nobody is there to switch it on, while the neuron is pushed down to 0.278% because MeCP2 is actively holding it shut. “Off by absence” and “off by repression” are different states, and MyoD makes the last point: it is abundant in muscle, it is a powerful activator, and it does nothing here, because this gene has no site for it to bind. Having the protein is not enough. The DNA has to have the address.

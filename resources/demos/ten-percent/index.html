What the pyramid shows

An energy (or production) pyramid. It can never be top-heavy — energy is lost as heat at every step, so each level must be smaller than the one below it.

10% is an average, not a law. Measured ecological efficiencies run from about 2% (many terrestrial systems, where most of the plant tissue is woody, hard to digest, or simply never eaten and goes to the decomposers instead) to about 25% (open-water plankton, where the food is small, soft and eaten whole). Nothing in physics or biology fixes the number at ten.

Left: how many times more zooplankton mass than phytoplankton mass is standing in the water at any instant. Right: even so, only about a fifth of what the phytoplankton build each year ends up as new zooplankton — near the top of the 2–25% range, which is exactly what you expect for plankton.

The biomass pyramid is upside down; the production pyramid is not. At any one instant there is more zooplankton than phytoplankton in the water. But the phytoplankton are eaten and replaced every few days, so over a year they produce far more new tissue than the zooplankton do. A pyramid of standing biomass is a snapshot and can invert. A pyramid of energy or production is a rate over time and cannot.

Standing-crop values are the classic English Channel figures (Harvey and colleagues, mid-20th century). Turnover times are rounded approximations and shift a lot with season and species, so treat the production row as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a measurement.

What this is for

The misconception, part one: students learn "10% of the energy moves up" and store it as a law of nature, like the speed of light. It is not. It is a rough average of measurements that scatter from about 2% to about 25% depending on what the food is made of and how much of it actually gets eaten. Slide the efficiency and notice that the bottom of the pyramid never changes at all while the top moves by orders of magnitude — a "small" disagreement about the rule is an enormous disagreement about the predators.

The misconception, part two: students also learn "pyramids are always upright" and then meet the open ocean, where a snapshot of the water holds more zooplankton than phytoplankton. Switch to the ocean view. The biomass pyramid really is upside down, because phytoplankton get eaten and regrown every few days — there is never much standing at once, but an enormous amount passes through. The production pyramid beside it — how much new tissue each group builds over a whole year, which is what energy flow actually measures — is still upright, and always will be: every transfer loses energy as heat, so no level can ever receive more energy than the level feeding it. Upright pyramids are guaranteed for energy and production, not for standing biomass.

What to notice: multiplying by an efficiency four or five times in a row is exponential, not gradual. That is why food chains almost never run past four or five links — push the chain to 5 levels at 2% efficiency and the reserve cannot support even one top predator, no matter how productive you make the plants. It is also why eating lower on the chain feeds more people from the same field: skipping one transfer multiplies the food energy available to us by roughly 1 divided by the efficiency.

Where this model stops being true: it assumes one clean chain with a single efficiency at every step. Real ecosystems are webs, efficiencies differ at each link, omnivores sit between levels, and detritivores route a large share of the energy around the chain entirely. The head-count number also assumes every predator is a 20 kg mammal with an average field metabolic rate, no seasons, and no wasted kills. Use these numbers to see the shape of the relationship, not to make a prediction about a real reserve.

One broken checkpoint is not cancer. Every failure on this page produces a specific defect, and a single one of them is usually survivable, silent, or lethal to that one cell. A cancer needs several independent hits stacked in the same lineage — typically an accelerator jammed on (a growth-factor-independent oncogene), at least one brake removed (p53, Rb), and then a defect that makes further mutations pile up quickly. That is why cancers take years and why they are far more common in old cells than young ones.

Honest limits

What is simplified. This model cell has 6 chromosomes and one of everything, so the karyotype is readable; a human cell has 46 and thousands of replication origins. Cyclin concentrations are relative, on a 0–1 scale, not molar. Each cyclin is modelled as one pool made at a set rate and destroyed at another, and the real families overlap far more than four clean curves suggest. On the wheel, M phase is drawn wider than its true 1/24 share so that the spindle checkpoint marker is visible; the phase durations quoted in the readouts are the honest ones. Cohesin, securin and separase are collapsed into “anaphase starts when APC/C switches on”. A real cell held in metaphase for hours usually dies or slips out of mitosis rather than waiting forever, and real DNA damage comes in kinds — a double-strand break is not a thymine dimer — which the single “damage” number here does not distinguish.

What this is for

The misconception: the checkpoints usually arrive as three names on a diagram — G1, G2, spindle — each with a fixed position on the circle. Naming them and placing them is a different thing from saying what any one of them is checking, or what specifically comes out of the cell when it fails. Both of those questions tend to collapse into a single answer: cancer. That answer is wrong three times over. The three checkpoints test three completely different things, they fail in three completely different ways, and not one of those failures is by itself a cancer.

Break them one at a time and read the daughter cell. Set damage to 100% and leave everything else alone: the G1 checkpoint holds the cell for about eleven extra hours while p53 drives repair, and it enters S with about 2% of the genome still damaged. Now untick p53 and run it again. The cell enters S at the normal 11 h with roughly 76% of the genome still carrying lesions, the replication forks copy straight over them, and those lesions stop being damage — they become permanent base changes that no checkpoint can ever see again, in both daughters. That is what “the G1 checkpoint failed” means: not cancer, but mutations fixed into the sequence.

The G2 checkpoint is a different question entirely. Turn replication stress to 100% and leave the checkpoint on: S phase stretches from 8 h to about 24 h and the cell simply waits, because cyclin B has already passed its threshold but Chk1 is keeping CDK1 switched off. Now untick the G2 checkpoint. Cyclin B reaches the same threshold at the same time, nothing is holding CDK1 down, and the cell tears into mitosis with roughly 43% of its DNA replicated. Unreplicated chromosomes cannot be pulled apart intact; they shatter. The daughters are missing genes outright. Nobody was checking for spindle attachment here, and no chromosome miscounted — the failure was about finishing the copy.

The spindle checkpoint is not a DNA checkpoint at all. It is the one most readily absorbed into the other two, because all three are drawn on the same circle and the first two really do watch DNA. This one acts at metaphase, and what it tests is mechanical: is every single kinetochore captured by microtubules, from both poles, and under tension? It does not look at DNA damage and it does not look at replication. One unattached kinetochore is enough to keep the mitotic checkpoint complex switched on, which keeps APC/C off, which keeps securin and cyclin B intact, which keeps sister chromatids glued together. Turn spindle problems up with the checkpoint on and the cell parks in metaphase — that is exactly how spindle drugs kill dividing cells. Untick the checkpoint and anaphase fires on schedule anyway: whichever chromosomes had not attached yet send both sisters to the same pole, and you get daughters with 7 and 5 chromosomes instead of 6 and 6. That is aneuploidy, and it is a different disaster from either of the other two.

Watch the cyclins, because they are the mechanism. Nothing on this page runs on a timer. Cyclin D is made only while growth factors are present, and it is what frees cyclin E–CDK2 by mopping up p27. Cyclin E accumulating past its threshold is what starts S phase, which is why removing growth factors early sends the cell back to G0 but removing them after the restriction point does not stop it — drag the growth-factor slider to zero at 5 h and then try again at 9 h. Cyclin A drives the forks. Cyclin B builds all the way through S and G2, and the moment CDK1–cyclin B activity is allowed to cross its threshold, mitosis begins. Every checkpoint on this page works by interfering with a cyclin–CDK complex: p21 blocks CDK2, Chk1 keeps CDK1 phosphorylated and inactive, and the mitotic checkpoint complex blocks the enzyme that destroys cyclin B. Notice what that last one means — the cell leaves mitosis because cyclin B is destroyed, not because a timer ran out.

And the accelerator is not a brake. The growth-factor-independent switch is a different kind of fault from all three checkpoints. The cell is not failing to notice a problem; it is manufacturing its own “divide” instruction. Set growth factors to zero with it ticked and the cell cycles happily with no signal from any neighbour. On its own, in a cell whose checkpoints all still work, this produces extra normal cells — a lump, not a cancer. Tick it together with p53 loss and watch the tally: now every cycle is both unwanted and mutagenic, and the mutations compound. That combination, over many divisions, is the road to cancer. A single mutation is not.

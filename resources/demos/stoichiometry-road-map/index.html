Numbers used

Atomic masses are the IUPAC 2021 conventional values (H 1.008, C 12.011, N 14.007, O 15.999, Cl 35.45, Fe 55.845, and so on). Avogadro's number is taken as  particles per mole; the defined value is 6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1.

What this is for

Where it goes wrong: "grams → moles → moles → grams" is usually learned as a chant, and a chant gives you no reason to ask what the middle step is for. Drop it and you divide by one molar mass and multiply by the other — a reasonable-looking calculation that has quietly assumed the mole ratio is 1 : 1. Keep it but write it upside down and the arithmetic still returns a number, just not the one you were asked for. Build it out of subscripts (the small numbers inside a formula) rather than coefficients (the numbers in front) and you have answered a question about a different substance.

What to notice: every step on this road stays inside one substance except one. Dividing by molar mass changes the unit but not the substance — you still have methane. Multiplying by Avogadro's number does the same. Only the mole ratio crosses the dashed line, and it can only be built from the coefficients of a balanced equation, because those coefficients are the only statement anyone ever made about how the two substances are related. Flip any fraction on the screen and watch what happens: the arithmetic still produces a number, but the units stop cancelling and you are left holding g × g/mol instead of grams. That is the check that catches an inverted factor every time, and it costs nothing.

Where the model stops: Level 4 assumes the substance you start with is completely consumed — that it is the limiting reactant, that the reaction goes all the way, and that the yield is 100 percent. Real reactions rarely oblige on all three counts, which is exactly why limiting reactant and percent yield are the next two topics. Also notice in the full-reaction table that mass totals match on both sides but mole totals usually do not. Atoms are conserved. Molecules are not.

Honest limits

The frequency is wildly inflated. On a real plate the mutation to phage or antibiotic resistance turns up in something like one cell in 107 to 109, so a real master plate of a few hundred colonies yields one or two resistant ones, not four. The slider starts at 10% so the pattern is visible on a dish you can see. Nothing about the argument depends on the number — drag the frequency down and the agreement columns barely move.

What else is simplified. Colonies are on a tidy grid; real plates are messy and matching positions takes a marked reference point on the dish. Each colony is treated as either resistant or not, when a real colony is a mixed population and the mutation may have arisen part-way through its growth. Selection is treated as perfect and instant: every resistant colony grows, every sensitive one does not, and no colony grows slowly. The three replicas are independent presses of one velvet. Resistance here is a single mutation with no fitness cost when antibiotic is absent, which is why the mutants are invisible on the master plate; many real resistance mutations do carry a cost.

What this is for

The hypothesis worth taking seriously. It is natural to read antibiotic resistance as something the antibiotic produces: the population meets the drug, and the challenge is what changes the cells. Saying a population “adapted”, “built up resistance” or “developed immunity” all describe that mechanism. It is a real hypothesis, it makes a sharp prediction, and it is the one simulated in the middle row. A piece of velvet is enough to find out whether the prediction holds.

Why the experiment works. The two hypotheses cannot be separated by counting. Set the frequency slider anywhere you like: both rows produce about the same number of resistant colonies per plate, because the same frequency feeds both. What they disagree about is where. If the antibiotic is what creates resistance, then each replica plate is its own separate event, and the positions on plate 1 tell you nothing about plate 2 — the predicted agreement is a fraction of one percent at the default frequency, and at most a few percent anywhere on the slider. If the mutation was already sitting in one colony on the master plate, back when there was no antibiotic anywhere, then the velvet carries that same colony to all three plates and the resistant spots land on top of each other every time. Only one of those pictures matches the observed plates.

What to notice. Press New run again and again. The middle row scatters differently every time; the bottom row and the observed row keep landing in the same places as each other. Then tick Reveal the master plate genotypes: the colonies that survive were flagged before any antibiotic existed, and on the master plate they look completely ordinary. Then push velvet transfer misses up to 20% — the agreement drops, because the velvet sometimes fails to carry a colony across, but the predicted figure only falls to about 52%, still more than two hundred times the induced-resistance prediction. The conclusion is not fragile.

Say it carefully. The mutation is undirected. It happened during ordinary growth on a plate with no antibiotic on it, from a copying error, and it was not caused by, aimed at, or produced in response to anything. The bacterium was not trying to survive, and had no way of knowing an antibiotic was coming. The antibiotic does exactly one thing: it kills the cells that lack the mutation. Variation first, by chance; selection afterwards, by consequence. Every time you catch yourself saying a population “developed” resistance, swap it for “the resistant ones were the only ones left” and check whether the sentence still means what you wanted.

Where this comes from. Luria and Delbrück (1943) got at it statistically, from the wild variability in how many resistant cells different cultures contained. Joshua and Esther Lederberg (1952) got at it visually, with sterile velvet and replica plates — the experiment on this page — and could then pick a resistant colony off the antibiotic-free master plate, a colony that had never once met the drug.

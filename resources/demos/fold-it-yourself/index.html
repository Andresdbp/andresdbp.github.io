Cooling brings it back. The melting curve is an equilibrium, so sliding the temperature up past 0.5 and back down to 0.2 really does refold the chain — try it. That is Anfinsen’s result: for a small single-domain protein the sequence alone carries the folding information. In a crowded cell, or a hot beaker, unfolded chains usually stick to each other and aggregate first, and aggregation is not reversible. That is a traffic problem, not a rule of physics — and it is why a boiled egg stays boiled.

Heat does not cut the chain. Every bead stays bonded to its neighbours at every temperature on this slider — look at the picture, the backbone never breaks. Denaturation undoes the weak interactions that hold the shape: hydrophobic packing, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds. The peptide bonds, and therefore the primary sequence, survive intact.

Honest limits

What is simplified. This is flat, not three-dimensional, and the beads sit on a square grid instead of anywhere in space. There are two kinds of residue instead of twenty. There is exactly one energy term — two H residues touching without being neighbours in the chain gain −1 ε — standing in for the whole hydrophobic effect, which in reality is driven by what happens to the water, not by attraction between the greasy groups. Water is not drawn as molecules; the empty grid sites are it. Backbone hydrogen bonding, and therefore all of secondary structure, is absent: there are no α helices or β sheets here. So are charge, disulfide bridges, chaperones, and everything about how fast folding happens.

What is exact. Within those rules, nothing is approximated. The lowest energy, the number of shapes tied for it, the melting curve and the melting temperature all come from scoring every one of the 220,375 shapes. The jiggling animation is a Metropolis Monte Carlo run whose long-run average reproduces those exact curves to within its own noise. One ε is worth roughly 1–2 kcal mol⁻¹ in a real protein, and T* is temperature measured in units of ε/kB, so T* = 0.3 is not 0.3 kelvin — it is the point where thermal energy is about a third of one contact.

What this is for

The idea being tested. “Primary structure determines tertiary structure” is a sentence you can repeat without ever being shown how a string of letters could decide a shape. Reading a fold as a picture to be memorised — settled by something outside the molecule — is a reasonable response to that, and it is what a labelled diagram of haemoglobin invites. On this page the deciding is the only thing that happens.

What to notice. Nothing on this page was designed by hand except the string of H and P letters. The shape is not stored anywhere. Press Anneal: the chain wanders, then collapses, and the six hydrophobic beads end up completely surrounded — average 0.33 of their four sides touching water, against 2.00 for every polar bead. Press it again. A new random seed, a completely different path, the same final picture. The sequence really does contain the answer, and you can watch it being found. Roughly one anneal in five stops short at −5ε instead of −6ε: that is a misfold, a chain trapped in a dead end because it was cooled faster than it could rearrange, and it is worth as much as the successes. Real cells employ chaperones for exactly this problem.

The honest catch, which is worth more than the slogan. Press Random sequence. That chain has 21 different shapes tied at its lowest energy, and annealing lands on a different one almost every run — in one test, 17 distinct shapes in 40 runs. Its sequence does not determine its structure. The difference is not luck: the designed sequence was selected, out of all 16,384 possible 14-letter sequences, precisely because it has one unique ground state. Real proteins are the selected ones. Four billion years of evolution has been throwing away the sequences that fold into whatever they feel like today, because a protein with 21 shapes has no reliable job. So the slogan is true of proteins, and it is true because of natural selection, not because a chain of amino acids must behave that way.

One letter. Press One letter → new shape: residue 6 goes from H to P, and the molecule has to refold. The backbone takes a different path, the best it can now manage is −5ε instead of −6ε, and the melting temperature drops from T* = 0.318 to 0.296 — the protein is less stable as well as differently shaped. Then press Sickle-style patch, which does the opposite — residue 2 goes from polar to hydrophobic, and the fold does not change at all. Same shape, same energy, same six contacts. What changed is that there is now a greasy residue sitting on the surface with two sides in the water, and the demo rings it in red. That is deliberately the real story of sickle-cell disease: the Glu6→Val substitution barely alters the shape of a haemoglobin molecule. It puts a sticky hydrophobic patch on the outside, and the patch lets one molecule dock onto the next, so deoxygenated haemoglobin S polymerises into stiff fibres that deform the red cell. One base change, one amino acid, no change in shape — and a disease.

And why proteins cannot fold by searching. This chain is 14 beads long and has 220,375 possible shapes, so your laptop can check all of them in a fraction of a second. Add residues and that number explodes: on this same flat grid a 100-residue chain has around 1042 shapes, and in three dimensions it is far worse. A real protein that tried each shape in turn would still be searching long after the sun burned out, yet it folds in milliseconds. That is Levinthal’s paradox, and the resolution is visible here every time you anneal: the chain is not sampling shapes at random. It is rolling downhill on an energy landscape, and the hydrophobic collapse you can watch happen in the first second is the first step of the roll.

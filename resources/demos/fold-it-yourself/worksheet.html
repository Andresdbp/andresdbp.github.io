1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. The point is to have a number on paper that the demonstration will either confirm or contradict.

Prediction

In this model a hydrophobic (H) residue that touches another H residue — without the two being neighbours in the chain — counts as one H–H contact, worth −1 ε of energy. The folded molecule has six such contacts.

Now heat it. At the hottest setting the page allows, T* = 3.00, the chain is thoroughly denatured. What number will the readout Average H–H contacts show? Commit to a value.

Write one sentence saying why you chose that number.

b. The two “sides facing water” numbers are averages out of a possible 4. Say in one sentence what the difference between them tells you about where the H residues have ended up, and where the P residues have ended up.

The demonstration is not fitting a curve or guessing. It scores all 220,375 ways a 14-bead chain can sit on a square grid and reports the best of them. Within the rules of the model, these numbers are exact.

a. Between which two of your temperatures does the folded fraction fall through 50%? Compare that interval with the Melting temperature readout.

b. Describe, in your own words, the shape of the folded-fraction curve: what it does over most of the temperature range, and what it does near the melting temperature.

c. Go back to your prediction in part 1. What did the readout actually say at T* = 3.00? If your number was different, name the assumption behind it that turned out not to hold.

d. Under Average H–H contacts the page prints a random-coil floor. Record that number, and explain why a chain thrown onto the grid at random still makes some contacts even though nothing is holding it together.

e. Leave the slider at 3.00 and press Run at this T. Watch the picture. Does the backbone ever break? Then slide back down to 0.20 with the simulation still running and describe what happens.

This melting curve is an equilibrium, so cooling really does refold the chain. In a crowded cell or a hot beaker, unfolded chains usually stick to one another and aggregate first, and aggregation is not reversible — which is why a boiled egg stays boiled. That is a traffic problem, not a rule of physics.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page, or look away from it. Show your working. Then reopen it, check yourself, and write down where any disagreement came from.

1. Colour the square grid like a chessboard. Residue 1 sits on a dark square; every step along the chain moves to a square of the other colour.

(i) Explain why two residues can be next to each other on the grid only if the number of chain steps between them is odd.

(ii) The designed sequence is HPHPPHPHPPHPPH, with H at positions 1, 3, 6, 8, 11 and 14. Using the rule from (i), and remembering that residues joined in the chain do not count as a contact, list every pair of H residues that could possibly form an H–H contact. How many pairs are there?

(iii) The demonstration reports a lowest energy of −6 ε, not the number you got in (ii). Explain why your count is an upper bound rather than the answer.

2. Each of the 14 beads has 4 sides. Two beads sitting next to each other on the grid hide one side from the water each. In the folded designed molecule the beads touch in exactly 19 places: 13 peptide bonds along the backbone and 6 H–H contacts.

(i) Calculate how many bead-sides in total are facing water.

(ii) There are 6 H beads and 8 P beads. Use your part-2 readings for Sides facing water, H average and Sides facing water, P average to get the same total a second way, and check that the two answers agree.

3. Two sequences differ at a single residue. Both have a lowest energy of −6 ε, both have exactly one shape tied at that energy, and the two shapes are the same shape. Explain, without doing any arithmetic, how such a substitution can still cause a disease. Name the property that changed and the physical consequence it has when many copies of the molecule are present at once.

4. This 14-bead chain has 2.2×105 possible shapes. A 100-residue chain on the same flat grid has roughly 1042.

(i) If a chain could test one shape every 10−13 s, how many seconds would it take to test all 1042? Compare that with the age of the universe, about 4×1017 s.

(ii) Real proteins of that size fold in milliseconds. Explain in one or two sentences what your answer to (i) rules out about how folding works, and what the demonstration shows happening instead.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The demonstration does considerably more than this worksheet uses.

  • Press Stretch it out, then drag the beads on the picture with your finger or mouse and try to reach −6 ε by hand. The chain will not let you break it or overlap it. How close can you get, and what makes the last contact hard to find?
  • Press Anneal (fold it) repeatedly. Each press uses a new random seed and takes a different path. Keep a tally of how often it finishes at −6 ε and how often it stops one contact short. A run that stops short is a misfold — a chain trapped in a dead end because it cooled faster than it could rearrange. Real cells employ chaperones for exactly this.
  • Press Random sequence, a sequence nobody selected. Read Shapes tied at that energy, then press the lowest-energy-shape button several times to step through them. Anneal a few times and see which one you land on. What does this sequence do to the claim that primary structure determines tertiary structure — and what does that suggest about how real proteins came to be exceptions?
  • Build a sequence with only one H residue, or with none, by clicking residues. Read what the melting curve says, and work out why there is nothing left to melt.

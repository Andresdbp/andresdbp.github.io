These two numbers are always equal. Not approximately — exactly, at every setting of every slider, including sizes far past the critical radius. They are computed independently here: one from k × volume, the other from Fick’s law at the membrane. In a steady state the surface must deliver everything the inside burns. The equations
D ∇²C = k,  C(surface) = Cs
C(r) = Cs − k(a² − r²) / (nD)
C(centre) = Cs − k a² / (nD)
acrit = √( n D Cs / k )
n = 6 sphere · 4 cylinder · 2 sheet

The geometry constant n is the whole difference between the three shapes, and it comes straight out of the Laplacian: a sphere draws in through a shell whose area grows as r², a cylinder as r, a sheet not at all. Reusing the sphere’s 6 for a sheet would be wrong by a factor of three in acrit². Past acrit the formula above would go negative, which is meaningless, so the demonstration switches to the free-boundary solution: consumption stops where the oxygen runs out, and the dead core radius r0 is solved for numerically.

Where this model simplifies

Consumption is zero-order. Every litre of live cytoplasm burns oxygen at the same rate k regardless of how much is there. Real mitochondria follow saturation kinetics with a very low half-saturation constant (under 1 µM), so zero-order is an excellent approximation almost everywhere — but it means the dead-core edge here is a hard line, whereas a real cell tapers into hypoxia over a few micrometres.

Other simplifications, stated plainly. The cytoplasm is treated as uniform water: one D everywhere, no organelles, no membranes in the way, and no stirring — real large cells cheat by streaming their cytoplasm, which beats diffusion outright. Cs is pinned at the membrane, so the outside is assumed well mixed; an unstirred layer would lower it. The cylinder is solved for radial diffusion only, ignoring flux in through its two ends, and the sheet ignores flux through its rim — both are good once the elongation slider is above about 5×, which is why the slider starts there. The drawing is rescaled to fill the panel, so read the size off the scale bar, not off the picture. And oxygen is only one of the things a cell has to move: this is the diffusion constraint, not a complete theory of cell size.

What this is for

The misconception: students learn that surface area grows as the square while volume grows as the cube, so SA:V falls as a cell gets bigger — and then they explain the size limit by saying the cell “does not have enough surface to absorb the nutrients it needs”. That sentence is wrong, and this page is built to break it. Look at the two flux numbers in the Does the membrane fail? panel. They are equal. They are equal at 10 µm, at 120 µm, and at 1000 µm with a third of the cell already dead. The membrane delivers 100% of demand, always. A cell in trouble is not a cell whose surface stopped keeping up.

What actually goes wrong. To push oxygen inwards you need a concentration gradient, and the deeper you want it to go, the more concentration you have to spend getting it there. Solve the steady state and the arithmetic is brutally simple: the centre sits at Cs − ka²/(nD). The drop is proportional to the square of the distance from the surface to the middle. Double the radius and you need four times as much concentration in hand at the membrane. At some point you do not have it, the centre reaches zero, and everything beyond that point is anoxic no matter how much surface the cell has. That radius is acrit = √(nDCs/k), and it is the real limit.

Watch the shape change do what SA:V cannot explain. With the starting settings, a sphere of radius 250 µm has a dead core 128 µm across — 13.5% of it is anoxic. Now tick keep the volume fixed and press Flat sheet. Not one molecule of cytoplasm was removed: the cell is now 55 µm thick and about 1.1 mm across, and its centre sits at 92.5 µM, essentially the outside value. Its SA:V rose by a factor of 3.4, but its centre concentration went from dead to full. Those two things are nowhere near proportional, because SA:V was never the cause. What actually changed was the trip from the surface to the deepest point: 250 µm became 27 µm, and the drop goes as the square of that, so the deficit fell by roughly eighty-fold. This is why genuinely large cells are long and thin or flat and wide — a nerve fibre a metre long is still only micrometres thick, and every point in it is a few micrometres from the outside.

Do not blame the clock. The other popular wrong answer is “diffusion is too slow to reach the middle of a big cell”. Check the timing panel. A real 10 µm cell equilibrates with oxygen in about 8 milliseconds; even a 100 µm giant does it in under a second, and a 1 mm one in a minute and a half. Compare that with a cell cycle measured in hours. The transient is over almost instantly on any biological clock — and once it is over, it stays over. The problem is not that oxygen takes too long to arrive. The problem is that when it gets there, in the steady state, there is none left. (Turn D down to 25 µm² s⁻¹ with the large protein preset and the timing does become serious — that is a real constraint too, just not the one that sets cell size for oxygen.)

The honest caveat, because it matters. With realistic oxygen numbers the critical radius comes out in the hundreds of micrometres — far bigger than the 10–30 µm cell you drew in your notes. So oxygen diffusion is not what stops your liver cell from being 50 µm wide; it has an enormous safety margin. What this criterion really explains is why tissues are threaded with capillaries every 50 µm or so, why a tumour spheroid grows a necrotic core at a couple of hundred micrometres (try the preset), and why the few genuinely huge cells that exist are either shaped like sheets and threads or are metabolically almost switched off, like an egg. The size of an ordinary cell is set by other things as well — one nucleus can only supply so much cytoplasm with mRNA, which is exactly why a skeletal muscle fibre big enough to see gives up and carries hundreds of nuclei.

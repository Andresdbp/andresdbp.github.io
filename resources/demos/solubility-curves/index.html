What this is for

The misconception, part one: students read a solubility curve as how much is dissolved. It is not. It is the ceiling — the most that can dissolve in 100 g of water at that temperature. Your sample can sit anywhere on the chart; the curve does not move to meet it.

The misconception, part two: "hotter dissolves more." That is a habit picked up from sugar and salt, and it is false for gases. Switch the solute to NH₃ and the curve runs downhill: heating drives dissolved gas out. This is why a warm river holds less dissolved oxygen than a cold one (about 14.6 mg/L at 0 °C, 8.3 mg/L at 25 °C, 6.4 mg/L at 40 °C in water open to air) and why a warm soda goes flat the instant you open it. Some solids do it too — cerium(III) sulfate is less soluble hot than cold.

What to notice: put the dot above the curve two different ways. Dump 110 g of KNO₃ into 100 g of water at 20 °C and you get a saturated solution with 78.4 g of solid sitting on the bottom. Dissolve the same 110 g at 60 °C and cool it gently with stirring switched off, and all 110 g stays in solution — supersaturated, with nothing on the bottom. Same dot. Different flask. Position on the chart alone cannot tell you which one you have; only the path can. That is why "supersaturated" is not something you can read off a graph, and why a supersaturated solution dumps its excess the moment you scratch the glass or drop in a seed crystal.

Where the model stops being true: these curves assume the solution is at equilibrium with pure solute and nothing else is dissolved in the water — add a second salt and the numbers shift. The NH₃ curve is for 1 atm of ammonia gas above the liquid and is tabulated only to 60 °C; halve the pressure and you roughly halve the solubility (Henry's law). Real supersaturated solutions are far more fragile than this demo makes them look — most collapse on their own long before you touch them. And the drawings of the flasks are schematic, not to scale.

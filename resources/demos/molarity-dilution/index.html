The definition, exactly

Molarity is moles of solute per litre of solution, not per litre of solvent. The V slider above is the volume of the finished mixture. In a lab you do not measure out 0.500 L of water and then add the solid — you put the solid in the flask first, then add water up to the 0.500 L mark. The solid takes up room, so those two recipes give different concentrations, and only the second one is 0.200 mol L⁻¹.

Where the formula comes from

Both lines above are the same sentence: moles of solute = concentration × volume. The top line counts the solute before you added water. The bottom line counts it after. Adding water cannot create or destroy KMnO4, so the two counts have to match — and “the two counts match” written in symbols is M1V1 = M2V2. The formula is a bookkeeping statement, not a new law.

Honest limits

Volumes are treated as additive here: 0.100 L of stock plus 0.300 L of water is called 0.400 L. For dilute aqueous solutions that is very close to true, but it is an approximation — mixing liquids can shrink or expand the total slightly, which is exactly why real dilutions are done in a volumetric flask filled to a mark rather than by measuring out solvent. The solubility ceiling of 0.40 mol L⁻¹ comes from 6.4 g per 100 mL of water at 20 °C (6.4 g / 158.03 g mol⁻¹ per 0.100 L = 0.405 mol L⁻¹), rounded. It is a temperature-dependent number: warm the water and more dissolves, which this model does not let you do. The colour on screen deepens with concentration and then stops when the solution saturates; it is drawn to be readable, not calibrated against a real absorbance measurement. KMnO4 is also an oxidiser that slowly decomposes in water and stains everything it touches; none of that chemistry is modelled.

What this is for

The formula is the last thing here, not the first. M1V1 = M2V2 usually arrives as something to memorise and substitute into, and a memorised formula is a thing that can be misremembered — inverted, used where it does not apply, with the two volumes swapped. Underneath it there is no formula at all. There is one physical fact: when you add water to a solution, the amount of solute does not change. Not a little. Not at all. Water is not solute.

What to notice. In Dilute it, set the stock to 0.400 mol L⁻¹ and take 0.100 L of it. The beaker holds 0.0400 mol. Now drag the water slider from 0 to 0.300 L. The concentration falls from 0.400 to 0.100 mol L⁻¹, the volume climbs from 0.100 to 0.400 L, the colour visibly fades — and the green moles tile reads 0.0400 mol the entire time. Count the dots in the beaker while you drag: the same dots spread over more space. Not one of them leaves. That is why the product M × V, printed on both lines, is the same number: it is the moles.

A second distinction: molarity is per litre of solution, not per litre of solvent. In Make a solution, the V slider is the volume of the finished mixture. Weigh out 15.80 g of KMnO4, add it to 0.500 L of water, and the result is more than 0.500 L of liquid, so it is not a 0.200 mol L⁻¹ solution. That is what a volumetric flask is for: solid first, then solvent up to the mark. The distinction is small for dilute solutions and large for concentrated ones, and it is the reason the definition says “solution”.

And concentration has a ceiling. Press Saturate it. You have added 0.200 mol of solid to 0.250 L, which would be 0.800 mol L⁻¹ if arithmetic were the only thing in charge. It is not: only 0.100 mol dissolves, the concentration stops dead at 0.40 mol L⁻¹ (the limit at 20 °C), and the other 0.100 mol — 15.80 g of it — lies on the bottom of the beaker as purple crystals. Keep dragging the solute slider and the number on screen refuses to move while the pile grows. A saturated solution is at equilibrium: solid is still dissolving, and dissolved ions are still crystallising back out, at exactly the same rate. Undissolved solid is not part of the solution, so it does not count toward the molarity — which is also why the dilution rule only ever applies to what is actually dissolved.

What this is for

The misconception: students learn that the equivalence point is where the acid and base have exactly cancelled, and conclude that the equivalence point is therefore always at pH 7. It is not. pH 7 is only where equal amounts of H⁺ and OH⁻ remain — which happens at equivalence only when nothing left in the flask reacts with water.

What to notice: titrate 0.100 M HCl with 0.100 M NaOH and the equivalence point computes to pH 7.00, because the leftovers are Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and neither one touches water. Now switch to the weak acid at the same concentration and volume. You add exactly the same 25.00 mL of NaOH — the stoichiometry has not changed — but the pH there comes out near 8.7. The acid is completely gone; what is left is its conjugate base, acetate, and acetate is a base. It pulls H⁺ off water and leaves OH⁻ behind. Slide pKa higher and watch the equivalence pH climb with it: the weaker the acid, the stronger the conjugate base it leaves, and the more basic the equivalence point.

The second half of the point: the half-equivalence pH is the acid's pKa, which is how pKa values are actually measured in a lab. And the indicator has to be chosen to match the curve, not out of habit. Put methyl orange on the weak-acid titration and it finishes changing colour around 2 mL — a 90 % error — because its range sits in the buffer region instead of on the vertical jump.

Where the model stops: every pH here is the exact solution of the charge-balance equation, but that equation assumes 25 °C, ideal behaviour (concentrations used in place of activities), one acidic proton, a 1:1 reaction and no dissolved CO₂. Real burette readings are also limited by drop size, roughly 0.05 mL. The on-screen indicator colours are a fair illustration, not a colorimetric match.

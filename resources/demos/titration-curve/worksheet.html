1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. Nothing here is graded on being right — the point is to have a committed answer on paper before the curve settles it.

Prediction

You titrate 25.0 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid (CH3COOH, pKa = 4.75) with 0.100 M NaOH. At the equivalence point — the moment every mole of acid has been neutralised — what is the pH? Give a number, not a range.

Now the same question for 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. pH at equivalence?

Write one or two sentences on your reasoning — specifically, what you assumed is left in the flask at the equivalence point.

2Guided setup: the strong-acid run

Open the demonstration. Build the strong-acid curve first, so you have a reference shape to compare everything else against.

  1. In the In the flask (analyte) panel, click Strong acid. The hint underneath should now name HCl.
  2. Set Analyte concentration to 0.100 M and Analyte volume to 25.0 mL.
  3. In In the burette (titrant), confirm NaOH (strong base) is selected and set Titrant concentration to 0.100 M.
  4. Before adding anything, read and record the Equivalence vol. and pH at equivalence boxes.
  5. Now add titrant with the +5.00 mL, +1.00 mL and +0.10 mL buttons to hit each volume in the table, reading pH now at each one. Check the Titrant added box to be sure you are where you think you are; Undo steps back if you overshoot.

3The core relationship: acid strength and the equivalence pH

Now switch the flask to a weak acid and change one thing only — how weak it is. Everything else stays fixed, so anything that moves is caused by pKa alone.

  1. Click Weak acid. Keep Analyte concentration at 0.100 M, Analyte volume at 25.0 mL, and the burette at NaOH, 0.100 M.
  2. Set the Acid strength, pKa slider to each value in the table. The hint underneath names a real acid when you land on one of these.

4Using it the way a question would

4A — Working backwards to a concentration. A titration is run to find an unknown, so the volume is the measurement and the concentration is the answer.

  1. Set the flask to Weak acid, Analyte volume 20.0 mL, Analyte concentration 0.150 M, pKa 5.50, burette NaOH 0.100 M.
  2. Before looking at the readout, calculate the equivalence volume on paper. Write it below, then check it against Equivalence vol.

a. Your calculated equivalence volume: ____________ mL    Readout: ____________ mL. Show the calculation.

b. From the Key points on this curve table, record the half-equivalence volume and pH. If this were an unknown acid and those were your only two measurements — equivalence volume and half-equivalence pH — show how you would recover both its concentration and its Ka.

4B — Where a habit breaks. A common shortcut is to treat indicator choice as a matter of preference, since any of them changes colour somewhere on an acid–base titration. Test that.

  1. Return to Weak acid, pKa 4.75, 0.100 M, 25.0 mL, burette NaOH 0.100 M.
  2. Work through the Indicator menu and record Colour changes at and Titration error for each one.
  3. Then click Strong acid (same concentrations and volume) and repeat.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Then reopen it, set up each scenario, and check yourself — if a number is off, write down where the method went wrong. Assume 25 °C, Kw = 1.00 × 10−14, throughout.

1. 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Calculate the pH after exactly 24.00 mL of NaOH has been added. Report to two decimal places.

2. 25.0 mL of 0.100 M of a monoprotic weak acid HA (Ka = 1.8 × 10−5) is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Calculate the pH (a) after 10.00 mL of NaOH, and (b) at the equivalence point. State which equilibrium controls each answer.

3. A 20.0 mL sample of a monoprotic weak acid of unknown concentration requires 30.00 mL of 0.100 M NaOH to reach the equivalence point. A pH meter reads 5.50 when 15.00 mL of NaOH has been delivered. Find (a) the concentration of the acid, (b) its Ka, and (c) the pH at the equivalence point.

4. Two flasks each hold 25.0 mL of a 0.100 M monoprotic acid — one strong, one weak. Both are titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Explain, without calculating, why the two titrations reach equivalence at the same volume but not at the same pH. Then state which of the two starting pH values is higher, and why.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded, and the demonstration does considerably more than this worksheet uses.

  • Tick Reveal the full calculated curve and press Run slowly. Watch where the recorded points bunch up and where they spread out, and work out what that spacing is telling you about the rate of pH change.
  • Put Strong base in the flask — the burette switches to HCl. Sketch the curve you expect before you add anything, then build it. Where is equivalence, and why?
  • Set the analyte to a strong acid and then click HCl (strong acid) in the burette as well. Read the warning that appears and explain, in terms of what is being neutralised, why Equivalence vol. shows a dash.
  • Drop both concentrations to 0.020 M with a weak acid at pKa 4.75. The equivalence volume does not move, but the vertical jump shrinks. Which indicators from Part 4 still land inside it?

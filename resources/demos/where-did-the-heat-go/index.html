Your hand feels cold. The water handed 2.57 kJ to the reaction, so the water — and your hand resting on the glass — ended up colder.

Simplification: we assume every joule the reaction takes in or gives off is exchanged with the 100.0 g of water, and nothing leaks to the glass, the air, or the bench. Real calorimeters warm their own container too, so measured temperature changes come out a little smaller.

What this is for

The tempting reading: an endothermic reaction “gets cold.” The evidence for it is real — the beaker really does chill your hand — but the sentence collapses two different objects into one. The reaction is the system and the water is the surroundings, and they do opposite things. In an endothermic reaction the system gains energy — it has to get that energy from somewhere, and the only place available is the water around it. The water loses energy, so the water cools, so the beaker feels cold in your hand. The cold you feel is the surroundings being drained, not the reaction “being cold.”

The other half of it: a negative ΔH is not “negative energy.” The minus sign is a direction, not a quantity. ΔH is defined for the system, so ΔH = −44.5 kJ/mol means 44.5 kJ of perfectly ordinary energy left each mole of system and went into the surroundings. Watch the two boxes labelled q system and q surroundings: they are always the same size and always opposite in sign.

What to notice: flip the toggle without touching the slider. The size of every number stays the same; only the arrows and the signs reverse. Then watch the box marked system + surroundings. It reads 0.00 kJ no matter what you do — that is conservation of energy, on screen, in the units of the problem.

Where the model stops: this treats the water bath as the only surroundings and gives the glass, the thermometer and the air a heat capacity of zero. It also assumes the reaction goes to completion. In a real coffee-cup calorimeter you would measure the calorimeter constant and correct for it, and the temperature change you record would be somewhat smaller than the one shown here.

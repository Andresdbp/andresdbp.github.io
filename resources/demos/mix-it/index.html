Split only what is genuinely dissolved and genuinely a strong electrolyte. The precipitate is a solid sitting on the bottom — it never separates into ions, so it keeps its whole formula in the complete ionic line. The same holds for gases, for liquid water, and for weak acids and weak bases such as HC2H3O2 or NH3, which stay mostly intact molecules in solution. Only strong electrolytes labelled (aq) get pulled apart.

What this is for

The misconception: two things students get wrong here, and both are about the middle line. First, most students think that pouring any two solutions together must produce a reaction — so they invent a precipitate that is not there. Second, when they write the complete ionic equation they split everything, including the solid they just made. Once the precipitate is broken into ions on paper, those ions cancel with the ones on the left, and the net ionic equation comes out empty or nonsense.

What to notice: watch the beaker after the solid drops. The spectator ions did not vanish, react, or leave — they are still swimming, in the same numbers and with the same charges as before you poured. That is the entire definition of a spectator ion: present, dissolved, unchanged. It is why they get struck out of the complete ionic equation, not because they are unimportant, but because they appear identically on both sides. Meanwhile the two ions that formed the solid are locked together in one block — that block is a single formula unit with an (s) label, and it stays whole in every equation line.

Where the model stops: the beaker draws about two dozen ions instead of 1022, and it pours exactly matched amounts so nothing is left over — a real lab needs a limiting-reactant check first. “Insoluble” is also an approximation: the Ksp panel shows that a tiny amount of every precipitate really does stay dissolved. And carbonates and hydroxides are strongly pH-sensitive, so adding acid can redissolve a solid that this demo shows as permanent.

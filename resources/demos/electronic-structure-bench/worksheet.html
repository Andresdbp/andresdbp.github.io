1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. The value of this page is having something on paper to argue with later.

Prediction

A magnesium atom has 12 electrons. Pull them off one at a time and measure the energy each removal costs. At some point the next electron will suddenly cost several times more than the one before it. After how many removals does that first jump happen? Give a whole number, and a rough guess at the factor (×2? ×5? ×20?).

Write one sentence saying what you reasoned from.

2Getting your bearings: where the electron is

Open the demonstration and stay on tab 1, Where is the electron? The left canvas is a slice through the nucleus, one dot per sampled position; the right canvas is the radial distribution. Four readouts sit under Numbers for this orbital.

  2. Press each orbital button in turn: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d z².
  3. For each one, copy the four readouts into the table. The Nodes (r / ang) box gives two numbers separated by a slash: radial nodes first, angular second.

Every radius in this table is for a hydrogen atom — the exact one-electron wavefunctions. A real sodium 3s orbital has the same shape and the same node count, but is pulled far closer to the nucleus by the larger nuclear charge. Do not quote 375.6 pm as a sodium radius.

4Where the quick answer fails

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Then reopen it and check yourself — and where you were wrong, write down which step went off.

1. An unknown element gives these successive ionization energies in kJ/mol: 786.5, 1 577.1, 3 231.6, 4 355.5, 16 091, 19 805. Work out the ratio of each value to the one before it. State after which removal the first cliff falls, how many valence electrons the atom has, and which group it is in. Given that it is in period 3, name it.

2. Write the full electron configuration of calcium. From the configuration alone, predict (i) after how many removals the first cliff appears and (ii) how many large multipliers appear in the complete chart of all twenty ionization energies. Justify each answer from the configuration, not from memory of the chart.

3. Using your node formula from part 2: which orbital in the tab-1 picker has exactly one radial node and exactly one angular node? Then, for a 4d orbital, give the number of radial nodes and the number of angular nodes, and the radius the Bohr model would assign to n = 4 (take a0 = 52.9 pm).

4. Boron has one more proton than beryllium, and sulfur has one more proton than phosphorus. Both have a lower first ionization energy than the element before them. Explain each drop. Your two explanations must differ from each other, and both must be consistent with increasing nuclear charge.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The demonstration holds more than this worksheet used.

  • In tab 1, compare 3d xz with 3d z². The clouds look nothing alike and the graph on the right is identical. Work out why, then predict whether 2p and 2s would do the same.
  • In tab 2, hunt for the element between Li and Ca with the largest first-cliff Cost multiplier. Before you check, argue from configurations which one it ought to be.
  • In tab 3, step through Sc to Zn and read the note each time. First ionization energy barely moves across the whole d-block. Say what that flatness implies about which sub-shell the outermost electron is sitting in.
  • Use Hide again in tab 2 to quiz someone else: name an element, have them commit to a cliff position, then reveal.

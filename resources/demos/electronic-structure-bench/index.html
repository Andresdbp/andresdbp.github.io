Where the simple story stops

The clean two-dip pattern is a main-group story. Across the d-block (Sc–Zn) first ionization energy is nearly flat and wobbles for its own reasons, and by period 4 the group-16 dip has shrunk to 3 kJ/mol — about 0.3%. A real trend can be real and still be small.

What this is for

The misconception: most students take “electrons are arranged in shells, 2 then 8 then 8” on the teacher’s word, and picture those shells as solid tracks the electron runs along, like planets. Two wrong things are bundled together there: that the shells are a rule someone made up, and that an electron has a definite location we simply draw as a circle.

What to notice in tab 1: the cloud is densest at the nucleus for a 1s electron, yet the graph beside it peaks at 52.9 pm. Both are true, and they are not the same quantity: the dots show probability per unit volume, the graph shows probability per unit distance, which multiplies by the surface area 4πr² of the shell at that radius. Press Draw the cloud again: the individual dots move, the shape does not. That is what a probability distribution is. The Bohr overlay is a single thin circle — a claim that the electron is exactly there. The cloud says the electron is found well inside and well outside it, and 3s has two spherical surfaces where it is found never. A planet does not do that.

What to notice in tab 2: nobody put the shells into this graph. These are measured energies. Sodium’s second electron costs 9.2× its first; magnesium’s third costs 5.3× its second; aluminium’s fourth costs 4.2× its third. The cliff moves one place to the right each time you move one place to the right in the periodic table, and it lands exactly at the count of valence electrons every single time. The shells are the conclusion, not the assumption.

One trap in tab 2, on purpose: the first cliff marks the valence/core boundary, but it is not always the tallest one. For aluminium through argon and for calcium, the deeper break into the 1s shell is a bigger multiplier than the first cliff. That is not a flaw in the idea — it is more of the same idea. Every big multiplier in the chart is a shell boundary; small multipliers are sub-shell boundaries or just the next electron in the same sub-shell. Count the big ones: an element with three occupied shells has two of them.

What to notice in tab 3: first ionization energy does not climb smoothly across a period. It falls at group 13 and it falls again at group 16, in period 2 and again in period 3, in the same two places. Drawing the trend as a straight rising line would teach a false pattern. Group 13 dips because the electron being removed is the first one in a new p sub-shell, which sits higher in energy than the filled s sub-shell just below it. Group 16 dips because it is the first p electron forced to double up in an orbital that already holds one, and two electrons crammed into the same orbital repel. (Chemists writing at a higher level describe that second dip in terms of exchange energy rather than simple repulsion; the pairing account is the standard one at this level and gets the direction right.)

Sources and caveats. Ionization energies are experimental values in kJ/mol from the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics (“Ionization Energies of Atoms and Atomic Ions”), the same table Wikipedia’s molar ionization energy list reproduces; no number in tabs 2 or 3 was invented or fitted. Orbital shapes are the exact analytic hydrogen wavefunctions, so the radii shown apply to hydrogen only — a real sodium 3s orbital is far more compact. Ionization order for elements 3–20 is the experimental one: 4s leaves before 3p, which leaves before 3s. Nothing here is animated to look good; every curve and every bar is computed on load.

Honest limits

K is not a property of the place. It is the population that the current food, water, nesting sites, predators and disease can support — change any of those and K changes, which is why the K slider exists at all. A drought can halve K overnight while the population is still sitting at the old value, and that, not a lag, is the other common route to a crash.

What is simplified. One species, one limiting resource, no age structure, no predators, no seasons, no immigration or emigration, and r held constant. Real populations are also stochastic: this model is perfectly deterministic, so the same sliders always give exactly the same curve, and small populations here never go extinct by bad luck. The lagged model is Hutchinson’s delayed logistic; at large rτ its crashes are far deeper than anything a real population survives.

Numerics: fourth-order Runge–Kutta, Δt = 0.005 day.

What this is for

The first misconception: that carrying capacity is a hard ceiling — a lid the population runs into and cannot pass. Students draw the S-curve, label the flat top K, and conclude that going above K is impossible. Real populations go above K all the time. Reindeer put on St Matthew Island in 1944 grew to roughly 6,000 by 1963 on an island whose lichen could support a few hundred, and then crashed to 42 animals in a single winter.

What to notice. Leave the lag at 0 and the logistic curve really does stop at K — not because K is a wall, but because dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) goes to zero exactly when N reaches K. Nothing pushes it over, and nothing ever can: that is a fact about the equation, so any textbook figure showing the smooth continuous logistic curve poking above K is simply drawn wrong. Now drag the lag to 2.0 days. The population sails past K to about 1,276 — a 28% overshoot — and then falls back and settles. Take the lag to 3.5 days and it never settles at all: it cycles between roughly 260 and 2,130 forever. The reason is the whole point. With a lag, the population is responding to the crowding of τ days ago. At the moment it crosses K it still “feels” uncrowded, so it keeps reproducing, and by the time the crowding registers, the extra individuals have already been born.

So what actually sets K? Not the model. Food, water, nest sites, predators, disease — and those change. K is the level a population is pulled toward under current conditions, not a line painted on the habitat.

The second misconception: that a population grows fastest when it is biggest. It is an easy mistake, because on the upper plot the curve is steepest somewhere in the middle, and students read “steep” as “lots of individuals”. The lower plot settles it. With r = 0.50 d⁻¹ and K = 1,000, the growth rate peaks at 125 individuals per day, and it peaks at N = 500, exactly half of K. A population of 900 grows at 45 per day. A population of 100 — nine times smaller — also grows at 45 per day, exactly. A population of 990 adds fewer than 5 individuals a day. Bigger is emphatically not faster.

Why K/2. dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) is a product of two things pulling in opposite directions: how many individuals there are to reproduce (N, which grows) and how much room each one has (1 − N/K, which shrinks). At small N there are plenty of resources but almost nobody to breed; near K everybody is breeding but there is no room. The product is largest where the two are balanced, and for a parabola through zero at N = 0 and N = K, that is the midpoint. Setting the derivative to zero gives r(1 − 2N/K) = 0, so N = K/2 and the peak height is rK/4 — every time, for every population. This is not a curiosity: it is why fisheries are managed for maximum sustainable yield at about half the unfished stock. Fish a population down to half and it replaces itself faster than if you had left it alone.

One honest caveat about the lower plot. The parabola is the growth rate of the no-lag logistic. Turn the lag on and the coloured orbit leaves the parabola completely, looping around it: with a lag, today's growth rate is not a function of today's population at all, which is exactly why the population can be above K and still growing.

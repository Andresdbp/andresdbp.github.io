4Working backwards, and one rule that breaks

Exam questions rarely hand you r and K and ask for the curve. More often they hand you something measured and ask for r or K.

  1. A population is observed to grow fastest at 90 individuals per day, and that fastest growth happens when the population is 400. Work out K and r on paper first, using Npeak = K/2 and (dN/dt)max = rK/4.

Overshoot in this model comes from the delay and from nothing else. Read the Honest limits panel: a real crash can also come from K itself dropping — a drought or a disease can halve K in a season while the population is still sitting at the old number.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away. Show your working. Then reopen it, check yourself, and where you were wrong write down which step went off.

1. A population has r = 0.60 d⁻¹ and K = 1,500 individuals. Find the population size at which it grows fastest, and the growth rate there. Give units.

2. A population has r = 0.25 d⁻¹ and K = 2,000. Find dN/dt when N = 600. Then, without doing the arithmetic again, name a different population size with exactly the same dN/dt, and justify it in one line.

3. Fisheries are often managed for maximum sustainable yield at roughly half the unfished stock. Using the two competing factors in rN(1 − N/K), explain why a stock held near K/2 can replace itself faster than one left near K. No calculation. Then name one thing this model leaves out that a real fishery would have to account for.

4. A population has r = 0.50 d⁻¹ and a reproductive lag of τ = 0.6 days. Compute rτ and predict, using the thresholds 1/e = 0.368 and π/2 = 1.571, whether the population overshoots K. Repeat for τ = 3.5 days.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded, and the demonstration does more than this worksheet uses.

  • Set K = 1,000 with the lag at 0 and push Starting population N0 up to 1,400. Which direction does the curve run now, and what does that say about calling K a ceiling?
  • Widen the Time window to 200 days and watch Exponential ÷ logistic, plus the label saying which day the exponential leaves the chart. How large does that ratio get, and what does it say about how long an exponential model stays usable?
  • Turn a lag on, press Play, and follow the moving dot on the lower plot rather than the upper one. Where in the loop is the population above K and still climbing, and where is it below K and still falling?

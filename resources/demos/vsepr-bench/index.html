A lone pair is fatter than a bond. A bonding pair is pulled out between two nuclei; a lone pair is held by one nucleus only, so it spreads wider near the central atom and shoves harder. That is why the real angle sits below the ideal one, and why the angles in the ordering above always run lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond > bond–bond.

Honest limits

The picture is a projection. Your screen is flat and these molecules are not. A wedge means the group comes toward you and a dashed bond means it goes away from you, so the angle you could measure with a protractor on the glass is not the angle in the molecule. Every angle printed on this page is the true three-dimensional one. The arc drawn on the molecule is a real 3D arc, swung through space and then projected, which is why it looks like a slice of an ellipse rather than a circle.

What is simplified. The compressed angles here are the standard teaching values — about 2.5° of squeeze per lone pair on four groups, about 2° on three — and real molecules scatter around them: H2O is 104.5° but H2S is 92.1°, NH3 is 107° but PH3 is 93.5°. VSEPR gets the shape right and the angle only roughly, because it ignores how big the outer atoms are and how tightly the central atom holds its electrons. Groups are all drawn the same length; real bond lengths differ. Five and six electron groups (trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, see-saw, T-shaped, square planar) are beyond this bench and beyond the AP list. The lone-pair–lone-pair angles quoted for the four-group cases are model values chosen to keep the ordering honest; lone pairs have no position you can measure with a diffractometer, because there is no nucleus out there to bounce anything off.

What this is for

Where this goes wrong. A molecule looks like it ought to have one shape, so “water is bent” gets stored as a single unconnected fact. That reading is reasonable — bent is exactly what you would see if you could look at the molecule. It is also incomplete. Asked for the electron-pair geometry of water, “bent” is the wrong answer, and it leaves nothing to say about why the angle is 104.5° rather than 180° or 109.5°. Two different questions are being asked: where do all the electron groups go, and where are the atoms?

What to notice. Put the bench on H2O. Four electron groups, so the four of them push apart into a tetrahedron — that is the electron-pair geometry, and the dashed frame draws it. Two of those four groups are lone pairs. Lone pairs are electrons; they have no nucleus at the far end, so a photograph of the molecule, or a model kit, or an X-ray structure, shows nothing there. Untick Show lone pairs. Not one atom moves. The frame vanishes and what is left is three atoms in a V: bent. Same molecule, same instant, two true names. Now press NH3: the top line still reads tetrahedral, because four groups is four groups, but with only one lone pair the atoms that survive form a tripod under the central atom — trigonal pyramidal. Press CH4: four groups again, and this time nothing is invisible, so both names finally agree. Tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal and bent are the same electron geometry wearing three different numbers of lone pairs.

Where the missing five degrees go. A perfect tetrahedron has 109.5° angles, and water measures 104.5°. Tick Show the ideal (uncompressed) bond positions and you can see the gap: the faint lines are where the bonds would sit if all four groups pushed equally, and the real bonds are pinched inside them. A bonding pair is anchored by two nuclei and is drawn out into a narrow sausage between them. A lone pair answers to one nucleus only, so it billows out near the central atom and takes up more angular room. Two of those bullying each other from the top of the molecule squeeze the two O–H bonds into a smaller angle than they should have. Ammonia has only one lone pair doing the squeezing, so it loses about 2.5° instead of 5° and lands near 107°. Check the ordering readout in any molecule with a lone pair: the lone pair–lone pair angle is always the widest and the bond–bond angle is always the narrowest.

“Bent” is not one shape. Press SO2. Bent again — but this one came from three electron groups in a flat triangle, not four in a tetrahedron, so its ideal is 120° and it settles near 118°, nowhere near water's 104.5°. If “bent” is all you carry, those two molecules are indistinguishable and neither angle is predictable. “Trigonal planar electron geometry, bent molecular geometry, one lone pair, so a bit under 120°” is a statement with content. That sentence is what the question is asking for, and it has three parts for a reason.

Why any of this matters. If water were linear its two bond dipoles would cancel and the molecule would be nonpolar — no hydrogen bonding worth the name, no unusually high boiling point, no liquid water at room temperature, no ice floating, no life as we run it. The 104.5° kink is what makes the O–H dipoles add up instead of cancelling. Shape is not decoration on top of the chemistry; the shape is the chemistry, and it is set by counting electron groups, not by counting atoms.

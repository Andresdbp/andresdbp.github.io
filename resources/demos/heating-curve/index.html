What this is for

The misconception: students believe that adding heat always makes something hotter, so a flat stretch on this graph reads as “somebody turned the burner off.” The burner is never off here. Energy pours in at exactly the same rate the whole way across, and for long stretches the thermometer does not move at all.

What to notice: temperature is a measure of how fast particles are moving on average. Watch the speed meter under the particle box while you cross a flat part — the bar sits exactly on the black tick and does not budge, even though the picture above it changes completely. The energy is going somewhere real: it is pulling particles away from each other against the attractions holding them together. Positions change; speeds do not. On the sloped parts the opposite happens — the arrangement stays put and the speeds climb.

Read the widths, not just the shape: boiling is the widest segment on purpose. For water it takes 2260 J/g to boil but only 334 J/g to melt and 418 J/g to go from ice-cold liquid all the way to 100 °C. Boiling costs about 6.8× melting and 5.4× the entire liquid ramp, so it eats about 73% of the horizontal axis. Textbook drawings that show two equal-width plateaus are teaching a false sense of scale. Across the whole trip only about 17% of the energy went into raising the temperature; the other 83% went into the two phase changes.

Where the model stops being true: this assumes 1 atm, a pure sample, and a perfectly even heater with no energy lost to the room, so the whole sample changes phase at once. Real beakers boil unevenly and lose heat to the air. The specific heats also drift a little with temperature; the three values here are the standard constants. And the particle box is schematic — it shows 60 particles instead of 1023, and it cannot honestly show how much emptier a real gas is than a liquid.

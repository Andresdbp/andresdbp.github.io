1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. Nothing is being checked yet — the point is to have something on paper that the demonstration can later settle.

Prediction

A 100 W hot plate is heating 20 g of ice. It is never switched off: 100 J arrives every second, all the way through. Follow the stretch that begins when the first drop of liquid appears and ends when the last of the ice is gone. Over that stretch, what happens to (a) the thermometer reading and (b) the average speed of the particles?

In one sentence, say what reasoning led you to that.

4Using it the way a question would

A. A shortcut, and where it breaks. A reasonable first move on “heat 20 g of water from −25 °C to 125 °C” is one line of q = mcΔT. Try it and see what it costs you.

  1. On paper, calculate q = mcΔT using m = 20 g, c = 4.18 J/g·°C and ΔT = 150 °C. Show the arithmetic.
  2. With the mass still at 20 g, drag to the far right end and read Total in.

a. By what factor does the shortcut fall short? Name every term it left out, and state what quantity each of those terms needs instead of c and ΔT.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page or look away from it. Show your working. Then reopen it, run the check written under each problem, and if you disagree with the demonstration write down where your working went off.

Constants, as listed in the demonstration, at 1 atm: cice = 2.09 J/g·°C  ·  cwater = 4.18 J/g·°C  ·  csteam = 2.01 J/g·°C  ·  ΔHfus = 334 J/g at 0 °C  ·  ΔHvap = 2260 J/g at 100 °C

1. How much energy does it take to bring 45.0 g of ice from −25.0 °C to liquid water at 0.0 °C? Give your answer in kJ.

Check: set the mass to 45 g and drag until State has just changed from Ice + water, melting to Liquid water. Read Total in.

2. A 100 W heater delivers all of its energy to 20.0 g of liquid water that is already at exactly 100.0 °C. How long until the last of it has become steam? Give the answer in seconds and in minutes.

Check: at 20 g, read Time on a 100 W heater at the start of the boiling plateau and again at its end, and subtract.

3. Find the total energy needed to take 15.0 g of liquid water at 20.0 °C to steam at 110.0 °C. Write each term separately before adding.

Check: at 15 g, record Total in with the dot as close as you can get to 20.0 °C liquid water, then again as close as you can get to 110.0 °C steam, and subtract. Landing exactly on those temperatures is not possible, so expect agreement to a few tenths of a kJ, not better.

4. Two identical 100 W plates. Plate A holds 20 g of ice sitting at exactly 0 °C; plate B holds 20 g of liquid water sitting at exactly 100 °C. Both samples change phase completely. Which finishes first, and by what factor? Then explain the difference in terms of what the energy has to do to the attractions between particles in each case — not by quoting the two constants.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The demonstration does more than this worksheet used.

  • While you are on the first flat part, the equation line under Right now reports how many grams have melted so far. Stop where exactly half the sample has melted and check This step against your own (m/2)(ΔHfus).
  • Set Mass of water to 1 g, then to 100 g. Does any figure in the Share column move? What does that tell you about what the shape of the curve depends on, and what it does not?
  • The Constants used panel says both ΔH values come from the molar figures 6.01 kJ/mol and 40.7 kJ/mol divided by 18.02 g/mol. Do those two divisions yourself and see how closely they land on 334 and 2260 J/g.
  • The caption under the particle box says a real gas is roughly a thousand times less dense than the liquid, and that the box cannot show it honestly. Work out roughly how much wider the box would have to be to draw the gas at true relative spacing, and decide whether the picture would still be worth looking at.

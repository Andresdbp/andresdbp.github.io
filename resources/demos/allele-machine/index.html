What this is for

The misconception, part one: "dominant" gets stored as stronger, and stronger gets stored as should take over. Press Rare dominant allele: A is completely dominant and sits at 2%. Run it for two hundred generations. It is still at exactly 2%. Now drag the dominance slider from top to bottom and watch what moves: the genotype bar does not change by a thousandth, and the p line does not so much as flicker. The only thing that changes is the bottom bar — who is indistinguishable from whom. Dominance is a statement about the phenotype of a heterozygote. It says nothing about how many copies of the allele exist. The Huntington's allele is dominant and rare; the allele that produces blue eyes is recessive and, in much of northern Europe, the most common one at its locus.

The misconception, part two: students describe Hardy–Weinberg as something populations do — "the population went into Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium." No population does this. Hardy–Weinberg is the prediction of what happens when nothing happens: it is a null model, deliberately built to be wrong, so that the size of its failure tells you which force is at work. That is why this tab is an infinite population with exact arithmetic rather than a simulation. Nothing here is noisy, so any movement in p is a force you switched on, not a fluke.

Worth noticing in tab one: switch on non-random mating by itself. The genotype frequencies leave Hardy–Weinberg immediately — heterozygotes collapse — and yet the change in p stays exactly 0.0000. Choosing who mates with whom reshuffles the alleles into different bodies; it does not add or remove copies from the gene pool. Four of the five broken conditions change allele frequencies. That one, on its own, does not.

The misconception, part three: "drift is random, so anything can happen and nothing is predictable" — and its partner, "drift only matters in tiny populations." Both are backwards. Follow one population on the drift tab: its path really is unpredictable, and no amount of biology will tell you where it ends. Then look at all two hundred at once. The fraction that ends up fixed at 1 lands on the starting frequency, give or take the few percent you would expect from counting only two hundred of them — press New replicates a few times and watch it hover there rather than wander off. The average heterozygosity falls along Ht = H0(1 − 1/2N)t almost exactly. The individual is random; the ensemble is a formula. And set N to 1000: heterozygosity still decays, just 1/2000 of it per generation instead of 1/50. Big populations are not exempt from drift — they are slow.

Where this model stops being true: one locus, two alleles, separate non-overlapping generations, a constant N, and only one force at a time on the Hardy–Weinberg tab. Real populations run selection, drift, mutation and migration simultaneously at thousands of linked loci, and the N that governs drift is the effective population size, which is usually a good deal smaller than the number of individuals you could count — unequal family sizes, skewed sex ratios and past bottlenecks all shrink it. Use these numbers for the shape of the relationships, not as a prediction about a particular species.

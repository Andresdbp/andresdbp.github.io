The signal arriving
10

This is the whole input to the cell: a handful of molecules landing on a handful of receptors. Nothing else is added anywhere downstream.

Bucket brigade sets every tier to ×1 — one molecule hands off to exactly one molecule, which is what the arrows in a pathway diagram look like they mean.

The four tiers — each one multiplies
×20

One hormone-bound receptor is an enzyme: it keeps swapping GDP for GTP on G protein after G protein for as long as the hormone stays bound.

×100

Each Gα·GTP switches on one adenylyl cyclase, which then makes cAMP continuously from ATP until GTP hydrolysis shuts it down.

×10

The net of two opposite things: it takes four cAMP to release the catalytic subunits of one protein kinase A, but each released subunit then phosphorylates many target enzymes. The number here is the net effect of both.

×1,000

Ordinary catalytic turnover: one phosphorylated enzyme converts substrate into product over and over until a phosphatase takes the phosphate back off.

These factors are illustrative orders of magnitude, not measured constants. They are the size that textbook cascades are roughly known to be — tens to thousands per tier. They are not values measured for one named pathway in one named cell, and no real cascade has a single fixed number per step. Treat them as “about this big”, and treat the total as an order of magnitude, not a fact.

Add a tier and you multiply by its factor. Add a step to a bucket brigade and you multiply by one, which is to say you gain nothing at all and just take longer.

Termination — how it stops
×1 normal

GTP hydrolysis by Gα, phosphodiesterase chewing cAMP into AMP, and phosphatases stripping phosphates back off. Each tier’s gain is catalytic rate × how long the activated molecule survives, so making the terminators faster shortens every lifetime and shrinks every factor at once.

What is simplified

Every tier here is a single fixed multiplier applied instantly, which is a cartoon of a real cascade in three ways. First, real tiers run simultaneously: cAMP is being destroyed while it is still being made, so the peak count is lower than a clean multiplication suggests. Second, nothing here runs out — a real cell has a finite pool of G protein, of PKA and of substrate, so every tier eventually saturates and the true output is capped well below what an unbounded product predicts. Third, the numbers assume every activated molecule works for the full lifetime and never fails. What survives all three simplifications is the only claim this page makes: the tiers multiply, so gain grows exponentially with the number of tiers rather than additively.

What this is for

The reading to test. A pathway diagram invites you to read signal transduction as a bucket brigade — one molecule hands the message to one molecule, which hands it to one molecule, and eventually the message reaches the end. The arrows genuinely look like handoffs, so the reading is a fair one. It just cannot be the whole story: if it were true, the four steps would be pointless, because a message that arrives unchanged after four handoffs would arrive just as well after one. Two answers to why bother with four steps come up quickly — “so the signal gets inside the cell” and “so it can be regulated”. Both are true, and neither explains the length of the chain.

What to notice. Ten hormone molecules land on the surface. Press Bucket brigade and the bars go flat: ten in, ten out, and every one of the five bars is the same length. Now press Realistic cascade. The bars march up the log axis a decade at a time and the bottom one reads 200,000,000 product molecules. The input never changed. Nothing was added at any tier. The whole difference is that each step is a catalyst, and a catalyst is not used up — one hormone-bound receptor turns on twenty G proteins, each of those switches on a cyclase that makes a hundred cAMP, and so on down. Four handoffs of ×20, ×100, ×10 and ×1,000 do not add to 1,130. They multiply to 20,000,000.

That is why the axis is logarithmic. On an ordinary axis the first four bars would be invisible specks against the last one — ten is one twenty-millionth of two hundred million. Each gridline here is a factor of ten, so equal spacing between the bars means equal multiplication between the tiers. When you drag a tier slider and its bar moves one gridline, that tier just got ten times stronger, and every bar below it moved with it.

And it has to be able to stop. A cascade this powerful triggered by ten molecules would be a catastrophe if it could not be switched off, which is why every tier has its own terminator: Gα hydrolyses its own GTP, phosphodiesterase destroys cAMP, phosphatases pull the phosphates back off. Drag Terminator activity to ×10 and the product falls from 200,000,000 to 20,000 — a ten-fold change in the terminators produces a ten-thousand-fold change in the output, because all four tiers lose their gain at the same time and 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. Amplification cuts both ways: the same multiplication that makes the cascade loud makes it fast to silence. The signal does not run until the hormone is used up. It runs until the terminators win, which is usually seconds.

The honest caveat. The per-tier factors on this page are illustrative orders of magnitude, chosen to be the size that textbook cascades are known to be. They are not measured constants for one named pathway, and the tiers in a real cell overlap in time and run out of raw material. Do not quote “200 million” as a fact. Quote the shape of the answer: a few molecules outside, millions of molecules of response inside, because four catalytic steps multiply.

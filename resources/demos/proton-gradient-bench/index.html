The gradient is mostly voltage. Textbook cartoons draw a crowd of H⁺ outside and an empty matrix, which makes it look like a concentration difference. It is not. A pH difference of 0.65 units is worth only about 38 mV; the other ~137 mV is charge separation across a membrane two molecules thick. Adding up the protons needed to charge the membrane to 137 mV changes matrix pH by about 0.004 units — the electrical part is almost free, which is exactly why it dominates.

Fuel and oxygen

NADH from the Krebs cycle enters at complex I (10 H⁺ pumped per electron pair). Succinate hands its electrons to FAD inside complex II, which pumps nothing, so that route only gets 6 H⁺. Switching donors is also how you find out what rotenone really blocks.

The equations being solved

pmf   Δp = Δψ − 59.2 ΔpH  (mV, 25 °C). At rest that is Δψ = −137 mV and ΔpH = pHmatrix − pHIMS = +0.65, so Δp = −175 mV. The tiles show magnitudes.
pumping   JH = 2 nH vO2, with nH = 10 H⁺ per NADH (4+4+2 at I, III, IV) or 6 per FADH2 (0+4+2).
back-pressure   vO2 = Vmax · f(fuel) · f(O2) · 1/(1 + e(Δp − 180)/22). The chain pumps against the gradient it built, so a bigger gradient means slower electrons.
synthase   Jsyn = Jmax / (1 + e−(Δp − 150)/10), 4 H⁺ per exported ATP (3 through the rotor, 1 for the ADP/ATP and phosphate carriers).
leak   Jleak = 1.48 (eΔp/40 − 1);   uncoupler   JDNP = 0.648 · [DNP] · Δp.

Honest limits

What is simplified. This is one averaged mitochondrion in a stirred cuvette, not a cell: ADP, phosphate and NAD⁺ are never allowed to run out, so ATP synthase is limited only by the pmf. Real respiratory control also runs through ADP supply. The oxygen number is the concentration in the medium and is held fixed rather than being consumed. The split between Δψ and ΔpH is modelled as relaxing toward a fixed ratio (59 ΔpH = 0.28 Δψ) set by the charge-compensating carriers — the phosphate carrier and the K⁺ cycle; a real membrane's ratio drifts with the medium. Rates are in nmol per minute per milligram of mitochondrial protein, the units an oxygen electrode actually reports, and the constants are chosen to land on textbook values rather than being measured from one preparation. Cutting the chain here simply stops ATP synthesis; in a real mitochondrion the synthase can spin backwards, hydrolysing ATP to defend the gradient, which is why cyanide poisoning drains cellular ATP faster than this model shows.

What this is for

The misconception: students say "the electron transport chain makes ATP." It does not. The chain is a pump. Everything it does with the energy in NADH is move protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space, building a force across the membrane. A completely separate machine, ATP synthase, is the only thing on this page that makes ATP, and it does it by letting those protons fall back. Two machines, one shared currency between them. Almost every hard question about respiration is really asking whether you have noticed that they are separate.

The experiment that settles it. Let the bench settle: O2 consumption 72, pmf 175 mV, ATP 331. Now press Oligomycin, which jams ATP synthase and nothing else. If the chain made ATP, blocking the synthase should leave the chain alone. Instead every number moves: pmf climbs to 220 mV, and oxygen consumption falls from 72 to 18. The protons have nowhere to go, the gradient piles up, and the pumps are pushing against a wall they built. Electron flow is not blocked; it is backed up. That is the whole idea of chemiosmotic coupling in one measurement.

Now the diagnostic. With oligomycin still on, drag the uncoupler slider to 100 µmol L⁻¹. DNP does not touch a single protein. It just makes the membrane leaky to protons. Oxygen consumption rockets from 18 back up to 130 — higher than it ever was with the synthase working — while ATP output stays at zero and the pmf collapses to about 40 mV. There is no way to explain that if the chain makes the ATP. Take the wall away and the pumps run flat out; they simply have nothing to show for it. The energy comes out as heat instead: the heat readout goes from 15 to 57 mJ min⁻¹ mg⁻¹. That is not a metaphor. Brown fat does this on purpose with a protein called UCP1 to warm a newborn, and in the 1930s DNP was sold as a diet pill for exactly this reason, until enough people cooked themselves to death that it was banned.

Three drugs, three different signatures. Rotenone and cyanide block the chain itself, so pumping stops and the gradient drains away: O2 consumption zero, pmf falling, ATP zero. Oligomycin blocks the synthase: pmf up, O2 consumption down, ATP zero. DNP blocks nothing: pmf down, O2 consumption up, ATP zero. All three kill ATP production. Only the direction the other two numbers moved tells you which machine was hit — and you cannot read those directions at all if the chain and the synthase are one thing in your head.

One more test, if you want it. Switch the electron donor to FADH2 (succinate, the Krebs-cycle step that feeds complex II) and press Rotenone. Nothing happens: rotenone plugs complex I, and succinate hands its electrons to complex II, downstream of the block. Cyanide still kills it, because complex IV is downstream of everything. Compare the two donors with no drug at all and you get the other half of the story: succinate makes the mitochondrion consume more oxygen (93 against 72) and produce less ATP (259 against 331). FADH2 skips the four protons that complex I would have pumped, so each electron pair buys 6 protons instead of 10 — a P/O of 1.39 instead of 2.30. Less back-pressure, faster electrons, less ATP. The proton count, not the electron count, is what sets the ATP yield.

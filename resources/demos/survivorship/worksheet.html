Survivorship: read the axis

What a survivorship curve is actually plotting, and why the same cohort can look like two different types depending on the y-axis.

1Before you open anything

Answer from what you already know. You are not expected to be right — the point is to have a number on paper to compare against.

Prediction

A survivorship curve is drawn as a perfectly straight line running from 1000 survivors per 1000 born at age class 0 down to 1 survivor at age class 10. How many of the original 1000 are still alive at age class 5, halfway to the maximum lifespan?

Write one sentence saying how you got that number.

2Read the life table before you read the picture

Open the demonstration. The plot is on the left; the Life table panel on the right tabulates whichever curve its tabs point at. One age class is one tenth of the maximum lifespan.

  1. Leave the buttons above the plot set to Logarithmic y-axis.
  2. In the Life table panel, click the Type II tab.
  3. Copy the listed rows out of the table columns Alive n(x), Surviving l(x) and Mortality q(x) into the first table below.
  4. Now click through the Type I, Type II and Type III tabs of the same panel and copy the four readout boxes sitting above the table into the second table below.
  5. Return to the Type II tab and press Linear y-axis above the plot. Look at the plot, then look back at both tables you just filled in.

3What makes a curve straight on a log axis

The panel Your species — the three types are a continuum draws a fourth curve whose shape you set. Moving the slider also points the life table and its four readouts at that curve, so the numbers follow you.

  1. Set the axis back to Logarithmic y-axis.
  2. Tick Draw a fourth curve.
  3. Drag the Mortality shape p slider to its far left end, where the value beside it reads p = 0.25. Record the three readouts.
  4. Repeat at p = 0.50, p = 1.00, p = 2.00 and p = 3.98. The Type II button underneath the slider snaps you to exactly 1.00.

4Working backwards, and one shortcut that fails

A question rarely hands you p. It hands you a mortality figure and expects you to place the species. Keep Draw a fourth curve ticked and the axis on Logarithmic y-axis.

  1. Move the Mortality shape p slider until Dies in age class 0 reads as close to 70.0% as the slider allows. Record p, the bend, and the sentence printed under the slider.
  2. Repeat until Dies in age class 0 reads 25.0%.
  3. Repeat until Median lifespan reads 32% of max lifespan.

b. Press Linear y-axis and leave Dashed straight-line reference ticked. The dashed line runs from 1000 survivors at class 0 to 1 survivor at class 10, and no curve on screen follows it. A cohort that did follow it would lose the same number of individuals every class: n(x) = 1000, 900, 800, … 200, 100 for classes 0 through 9. Calculate q(0) and q(8) for that cohort, showing your working.

c. Using your two values from part b, explain where the shortcut “a straight survivorship line means a constant death rate” breaks down, and what has to be added to it to make it true.

5Solve these without the demonstration

Close the page, or look away from it. Show your working. Then reopen it and check yourself — and if you were wrong, write down where the reasoning went off.

1. A cohort of 1000 loses exactly half of its survivors in every age class. Complete n(x) for x = 0 to 5. How many individuals die during class 0, and how many during class 4? Both are the same q(x) — explain in one sentence why the two counts differ so much.

2. A life table gives n(x) = 1000, 60, 25, 14 for age classes 0 to 3. Calculate q(0), q(1) and q(2) to one decimal place. Is the proportion dying constant? Which of the three types is this cohort closest to, and would its log plot be straight?

3. A figure in a paper shows survivors falling as a straight line from 1000 down to 1 across ten age classes, but the y-axis has no scale printed on it. Explain why the straight line alone does not tell you the species is Type II, and state the one piece of information that would settle it. No calculation.

4. Take the demonstration's Type II cohort, which loses 49.9% of its survivors in every age class starting from 1000. Calculate the first age class at which fewer than 10 of the original 1000 are still alive. Show the working, not just the answer.

Problem 1 gives a clean 50% per class and problem 4 uses the demonstration's 49.9%. That difference is not a rounding slip. Every curve in the model is forced to end at exactly 1 survivor per 1000 at the maximum lifespan, which fixes the per-class factor at 10001/10. Real cohorts are not forced to do anything of the kind.

6On your own

Nothing here is graded. The demonstration does more than this worksheet uses.

  • Watch the Mortality rate q(x) bar chart below the plot while you click through the four life-table tabs. Then raise p slowly from 1.00 and find the point at which the bars stop looking level to your eye. Compare that p with what the bend readout was already reporting.
  • The closing section notes that every curve is forced to end at exactly 1 survivor per 1000. Decide which of the four readouts would move if a species ended its maximum lifespan with 100 survivors instead, and which would not.
  • Untick every curve except Type III and compare where it meets the bottom of the plot on each axis. The log axis cannot display zero survivors and the linear axis can — work out what that means for reading a real cohort that does reach zero.
